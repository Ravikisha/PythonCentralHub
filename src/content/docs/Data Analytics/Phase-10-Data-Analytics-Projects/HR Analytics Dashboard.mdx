## Goal

From HR data, answer:

- Attrition rate
- Attrition by department/job role
- Salary and tenure differences

## Analysis pipeline

```mermaid title="HR analytics pipeline" desc="From raw employee records to attrition insights by segment."
flowchart LR
  A["Raw HR data<br/>(attrition 0/1)"] --> B["Rate<br/>(overall attrition %)"]
  B --> C["Segment<br/>(department, role)"]
  C --> D["Visualize<br/>(attrition by segment)"]
  D --> E["Conclude<br/>(interventions)"]
```

:::tip[From the book]
McKinney builds a two-way `pivot_table` (movie title x gender) instead of running
several separate `groupby` calls, because it puts both dimensions side by side in
one readable table. The same move works here: `df.pivot_table("attrition",
index="department", columns="job_role", aggfunc="mean")` shows every
department/role combination's attrition rate at once.
:::

function setup() {

  noLoop();

function draw() {
  background(13, 17, 23);

  line(gx, gy + gh, gx + gw, gy + gh);

  const rates = [0.28, 0.12, 0.20, 0.15];

    text(Math.round(rates[i] * 100) + "%", gx + i * bw + bw / 2, gy + gh - h - 16);
  });
}

## Deliverable

A dashboard should include:

- Attrition KPI
- Top segments with high attrition
- Suggested interventions (training, compensation, career path)


## Why attrition rate is a mean

Attrition is a yes-or-no outcome for each employee. If you store it as 0 for "stayed" and 1 for "left", then the mean of the column is exactly the share of people who left, which is the attrition rate. A mean of `0.16` means 16 percent left. This trick is used everywhere in analytics: a 0/1 column makes rates, conversion and churn one `mean()` away, and `groupby` turns it into a rate per segment.

Real HR files often store the column as the text "Yes" and "No". Convert it first, or the mean will fail:

```python title="Clean the target" showLineNumbers{1}
import pandas as pd

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "department": ["Sales", "Sales", "IT", "IT", "HR", "HR"],
    "attrition": ["Yes", "No", "No", "No", "Yes", "Yes"],
    "salary": [50, 60, 80, 90, 55, 52],
})

df["attrition"] = (df["attrition"] == "Yes").astype(int)

print(df["attrition"].mean())                        # 0.5
print(df.groupby("department")["attrition"].mean())
# HR       1.0
# IT       0.0
# Sales    0.5
```

## Reading the segment comparison

The overall rate hides differences between groups. A company at 16 percent overall may have one department at 4 percent and another at 30 percent, and only the second needs action. Group by each dimension you care about (department, job role, tenure band) and sort from highest to lowest so the segments needing attention appear first.

Be careful with small groups. A department with three employees can show 100 percent attrition after one person leaves. Always print the group size next to the rate:

```python title="Rate and size" showLineNumbers{1}
summary = df.groupby("department")["attrition"].agg(rate="mean", people="count")
print(summary.sort_values("rate", ascending=False))
```

## Interpreting the charts

`sns.barplot` with a 0/1 target draws the mean per group, which is the rate, and adds a confidence interval line. A long interval signals a small or noisy group. The box plot of salary by attrition compares the middle 50 percent of pay for people who stayed and left. If the "left" box sits lower, pay may be a factor, but that is a hint and not proof, since other variables such as role and tenure can explain the gap.

## Common mistakes

- **Averaging a text column.** Convert "Yes/No" to 1/0 first.
- **Ignoring group size**, and reporting big percentages from tiny groups.
- **Claiming a cause.** Lower pay among leavers does not prove pay made them leave.
- **Using different definitions of attrition** in different charts, such as voluntary in one and all exits in another.
- **Charts without labels**, which make a dashboard unreadable to managers.

## Turning it into a dashboard

Start with the headline KPI, then show the two or three segments with the highest rate, then the drivers (salary, tenure, overtime), and finish with recommendations. Every chart should answer one question that an HR manager would actually ask.
