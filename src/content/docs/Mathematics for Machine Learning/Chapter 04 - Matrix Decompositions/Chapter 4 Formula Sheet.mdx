## The one-line summary

Every decomposition in this chapter trades a condition for a capability:

$$
\underbrace{\text{SPD}}_{\text{Cholesky}}
\;\subset\;
\underbrace{\text{symmetric}}_{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^\top}
\;\subset\;
\underbrace{\text{non-defective square}}_{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1}}
\;\subset\;
\underbrace{\text{any matrix at all}}_{\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top}
$$

Read it right to left and the SVD is the one with no entry fee. Read it left to right and each step buys
something cheaper: Cholesky costs $\tfrac13n^3$ where an SVD costs about $20n^3$.

non-symmetric input: it reads only the lower triangle.

| — | $\prod_i\sigma_i = \lvert\det\mathbf{A}\rvert$ and $\sigma_1 = \lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_2$ | square for the first | — |
| — | $\sigma_i = \lvert\lambda_i\rvert$ **only** for symmetric matrices | symmetric | — |
| — | for symmetric $\mathbf{A}$ the eigendecomposition and the SVD coincide | symmetric | — |

**Never compute an SVD through a Gram matrix.** $\kappa(\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A}) = \kappa(\mathbf{A})^2$;
measured, the smallest singular value is $4\%$ wrong at $\kappa = 10^8$ and lost entirely at $10^{12}$.

## §4.7 Matrix phylogeny

| class | condition | what it gives you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| real, any shape | — | SVD, pseudo-inverse |
| square | $m = n$ | determinant, trace, eigenvalues |
| **regular / invertible** | $\det \neq 0$ | $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$ |
| **non-defective** | $n$ independent eigenvectors | $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1}$ |
| **normal** | $\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^\top$ | singular and eigen directions align |
| **orthogonal** (Def 3.8) | $\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^\top = \mathbf{I}$ | $\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \mathbf{A}^\top$, free |
| **rotation** | orthogonal with $\det = +1$ | preserves orientation |
| **symmetric** | $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{S}^\top$ | **real** eigenvalues, orthonormal eigenbasis |
| **positive definite** | $\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x} > 0$ | unique Cholesky, $\lambda > 0$, invertible |
| **diagonal** | $a_{ij} = 0$, $i \neq j$ | everything elementwise |
| identity | $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{I}$ | — |

:::danger[Non-singular and non-defective are independent]
A rotation matrix is invertible with complex eigenvalues; $\mathrm{diag}(1,0)$ is diagonal and singular.
All four combinations occur — Exercise 4.5 gives one $2\times2$ witness each.
:::

Diagonal matrices are **closed** under multiplication and addition but form a **group** only when every
diagonal entry is nonzero.

## The six mistakes this chapter is designed to prevent

1. **Computing a determinant by cofactor expansion.** $n!$ against $n^3/3$. Use elimination, or Cholesky if the matrix is SPD.
2. **Assuming invertible means diagonalizable.** Measured, the implication holds $85\%$ one way and $98\%$ the other. Neither is a theorem.
3. **Assuming a repeated eigenvalue means defective.** Exercise 4.6b has an eigenvalue repeated three times and is perfectly diagonalizable. What matters is $\dim\ker(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})$.
4. **Writing $\mathbf{P}^{-1} = \mathbf{P}^\top$ without checking symmetry.** Example 4.5 is diagonalizable with $\lVert\mathbf{P}^\top\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{I}\rVert = 0.3162$.
5. **Constructing an SVD through $\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A}$.** The condition number squares. The construction is a proof, not an algorithm.
6. **Reading individual eigenvector or singular-vector components across a repeated value.** Any rotation inside the repeated eigenspace is equally valid, so different builds report different vectors.

## Recall card

- **The chapter is a ladder of conditions**: Cholesky needs SPD, the eigendecomposition needs square and non-defective, the SVD needs nothing — and the cheaper the decomposition, the stricter the condition.
- **Determinant and trace are basis-independent**, so they describe the mapping rather than the matrix; both equal a symmetric function of the eigenvalues (product and sum).
- **The spectral theorem is the load-bearing result**: symmetric implies real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis, which is what makes Theorem 4.21, the SVD construction and PCA all work.
- **Diagonalizability is about the count of independent eigenvectors**, which is the sum of the geometric multiplicities — not about repeats and not about invertibility.
- **The SVD's two outer factors are orthogonal and live in different spaces**, so unlike P and P-inverse they are not inverses of each other; Sigma has the shape of A and pads with zeros.
- **Eckart-Young gives the error before you compute the approximation**: exactly sigma-k-plus-one in the spectral norm, and the square root of the discarded tail in the Frobenius norm.
- **Low rank is not few numbers**: a rank-k factorisation costs k(m+n+1), so the saving depends on the ratio of k to mn/(m+n+1).
- **Three library traps**: `cholesky` and `eigh` read only the lower triangle; `np.linalg.qr` returns a Q with determinant exactly (-1) to the (n-1); and `eig` returns P for a defective matrix without complaint.
