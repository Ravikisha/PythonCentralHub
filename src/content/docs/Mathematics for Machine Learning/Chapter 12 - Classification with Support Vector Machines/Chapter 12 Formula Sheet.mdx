## §12.1 Separating hyperplanes

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| the task | $f : \mathbb{R}^D \to \{+1, -1\}$ — **12.1** |
| the model | $f(\mathbf{x}) = \langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle + b$ — **12.2a**, **12.2b** |
| the hyperplane | $\{\mathbf{x} : f(\mathbf{x}) = 0\}$ — **12.3** |
| $\mathbf{w}$ is normal to it | $f(\mathbf{x}_a) - f(\mathbf{x}_b) = \langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_a - \mathbf{x}_b\rangle$ — **12.4a**, **12.4b** |
| correct classification | $\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle + b \geq 0$ if $y_n = +1$; $< 0$ if $y_n = -1$ — **12.5**, **12.6** |
| combined | $y_n(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle + b) \geq 0$ — **12.7** |

:::caution[Three things Section 12.1 leaves unsaid]
**Equation 12.3 names a set, not parameters.** Every positive multiple of $(\mathbf{w}, b)$ gives the
same classifier — measured identical at $c = 0.5, 1, 2, 17, 1000$ — so "distance to the hyperplane"
is meaningless until a scale is fixed.

**Equations 12.5 and 12.6 are not symmetric.** One is inclusive and one strict, so a point exactly on
the hyperplane is classified $+1$ by fiat, and Equation 12.7 is very slightly weaker than the pair it
replaces.

**Zero training error does not pick a classifier.** $9{,}628$ sampled hyperplanes separate the eight
points perfectly and their held-out accuracy spans $0.1119$.
:::

## §12.2.1 The margin

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| vector addition | $\mathbf{x}_a = \mathbf{x}_a' + r\,\dfrac{\mathbf{w}}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert}$ — **12.8** |
| both classes at once | $y_n(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle + b) \geq r$ — **12.9** |
| **the problem** | $\max_{\mathbf{w},b,r} r$ s.t. **12.9**, $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert = 1$, $r > 0$ — **12.10** |

$r$ is a **signed coordinate** along $\mathbf{w}/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$: measured on all eight
points, $\lvert r\rvert$ equals the distance to the projection to $0.000\times10^{0}$, and the sign
gives the side. With $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert = 1$, $f(\mathbf{x})$ **is** that coordinate.

## §12.2.2–12.2.3 Setting the margin to one

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| the projection is on the hyperplane | $\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_a'\rangle + b = 0$ — **12.11** |
| substitute 12.8 | **12.12**, expand by bilinearity — **12.13** |
| **the margin** | $r = \dfrac{1}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert}$ — **12.14** |
| the constraint | $y_n(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle + b) \geq 1$ — **12.15** |
| maximise the reciprocal | **12.16**, **12.17** |
| **the hard margin SVM** | $\min_{\mathbf{w},b}\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$ s.t. **12.15** — **12.18**, **12.19** |
| **Theorem 12.1** | 12.10 and 12.21 are equivalent — **12.20**, **12.21** |
| the proof's hinge | $\lVert\mathbf{w}''\rVert = 1/r$ — **12.24**, checked to $1.137\times10^{-13}$ |

:::tip[Theorem 12.1 is an upgrade, not just an equivalence]
The constraint $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert = 1$ confines $\mathbf{w}$ to a sphere, which is **not a convex
set**. Over $2{,}000$ random starts:

| formulation | failed to converge | distinct optima |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **12.10** | $251$ — $12.55\%$ | $1$ |
| **12.21** | $\mathbf{0}$ — $0.00\%$ | $1$ |

Every run that converged found $r = 1$, so the non-convexity costs reliability rather than accuracy.
Squaring the norm buys the same thing on the objective side: $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ failed $3$ of
$500$ starts and took up to $29$ iterations; $\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$ failed none and never
exceeded $5$.
:::

## §12.2.4–12.2.5 The soft margin, twice

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| **geometric view** | $\min \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + C\sum_n\xi_n$ s.t. $y_n f(\mathbf{x}_n) \geq 1-\xi_n$, $\xi_n \geq 0$ — **12.26a–c** |
| the hypothesis class | $f(\mathbf{x}) = \langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle + b$ — **12.27** |
| **the hinge loss** | $\ell(t) = \max\{0, 1-t\}$, $t = yf(\mathbf{x})$ — **12.28** |
| as two pieces | $0$ if $t \geq 1$, else $1-t$ — **12.29** |
| the hard margin's loss | $0$ if $t \geq 1$, else $\infty$ — **12.30** |
| **loss view** | $\min \underbrace{\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2}_{\text{regularizer}} + C\sum_n\underbrace{\max\{0, 1-y_nf(\mathbf{x}_n)\}}_{\text{error term}}$ — **12.31** |
| why they are the same | $\min_t\max\{0,1-t\} \equiv \min_{\xi,t}\xi$ s.t. $\xi\geq0$, $\xi\geq1-t$ — **12.32**, **12.33** |

| $\xi_n$ | where the example is |
| --- | --- |
| $0$ | correct, at or beyond its margin — costs nothing |
| $(0, 1]$ | correct side, **inside** the margin |
| $> 1$ | **wrong** side of the hyperplane |

:::note[What C does, and the remark that is not a remark]
$C$ is the price of a violation, and **large $C$ means low regularisation** — the reverse of the usual
convention, because $C$ multiplies the error term.

| limit | measured |
| --- | --- |
| $C \to \infty$ on separable data | recovers the hard margin exactly by $C = 1$, $\lvert b - b^*\rvert = 0$ |
| $C \to 0$ | $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert \propto C$; margin balloons to $800$; at $C = 0$ the classifier is constant |
| above $C \approx 2$ here | the solution stops moving; only the objective scales |

**$b$ is left out of the regulariser, and that is what makes the method invariant to where the origin
sits.** Shifting the data by $(10, 0)$ leaves the optimal value unchanged to $1.993\times10^{-15}$;
adding $\tfrac12 b^2$ to the objective changes it by $\mathbf{10.48}$ and shrinks
$\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ from $1.000000$ to $0.800000$.
:::

## §12.3.1 The dual

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| the Lagrangian | $\mathfrak{L} = \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + C\sum\xi_n - \sum\alpha_n(y_nf(\mathbf{x}_n)-1+\xi_n) - \sum\gamma_n\xi_n$ — **12.34** |
| the three derivatives | **12.35**, **12.36**, **12.37** |
| **the representer theorem** | $\mathbf{w} = \sum_n \alpha_n y_n \mathbf{x}_n$ — **12.38** |
| substituting back | **12.39**, **12.40** |
| **the dual SVM** | $\min_{\boldsymbol\alpha}\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}y_iy_j\alpha_i\alpha_j\langle\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j\rangle - \sum_i\alpha_i$ s.t. $\sum_i y_i\alpha_i = 0$, $0 \leq \alpha_i \leq C$ — **12.41** |
| recovering the intercept | $b^* = y_n - \langle\mathbf{w}^*,\mathbf{x}_n\rangle$ — **12.42** |

| the KKT conditions, which the book gives only as a margin note | |
| --- | --- |
| $\alpha_n = 0$ | $y_nf(\mathbf{x}_n) \geq 1$ |
| $0 < \alpha_n < C$ | $y_nf(\mathbf{x}_n) = 1$ |
| $\alpha_n = C$ | $y_nf(\mathbf{x}_n) \leq 1$ |

:::caution[Two things to correct in §12.3.1]
**The margin note runs only one way.** It says examples on the margin *are* those with
$0 < \alpha_i < C$. The third KKT row permits $y_nf(\mathbf{x}_n) = 1$ with $\alpha_n = C$, and at
$C = 2$ on the running example three on-margin examples sit exactly at the bound. The optimum there
is unique — the constraints force $4t - 3.5u = 1$ and maximising $\sum\alpha_n = 1 + 3.5u$ pushes $u$
to $C$.

**The fallback rule for $b$ discards a sign.** Taking the median of
$\lvert y_n - \langle\mathbf{w}^*,\mathbf{x}_n\rangle\rvert$ returns $+2$ where the answer is $-2$.
Drop the absolute value.
:::

:::tip[An identity the book does not state]
At a hard-margin optimum,

$$
\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_n = \frac{1}{r^2}
$$

**The total dual mass is the inverse squared margin** — verified to $4.782\times10^{-8}$ over $200$
random datasets. A wide margin means small multipliers, which is the dual saying what the primal says
by minimising $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$.
:::

## §12.3.2 The convex hull view

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| the convex hull | $\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X}) = \{\sum_n\alpha_n\mathbf{x}_n\}$, $\sum_n\alpha_n = 1$, $\alpha_n \geq 0$ — **12.43** |
| the difference vector | $\mathbf{w} := \mathbf{c} - \mathbf{d}$ — **12.44** |
| the objective | $\arg\min\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert = \arg\min\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$ — **12.45** |
| the two points | $\mathbf{c} = \sum_{y_n=+1}\alpha_n^{+}\mathbf{x}_n$, $\mathbf{d} = \sum_{y_n=-1}\alpha_n^{-}\mathbf{x}_n$ — **12.46**, **12.47** |
| in hull coordinates | **12.48** |
| each hull sums to one | **12.49**, which multiplies out to $\sum_n y_n\alpha_n = 0$ — **12.50**, **12.51** |
| the soft margin's geometry | the **reduced hull**, with every weight capped |

:::tip[The conversion §12.3.2 omits]
The hull vector and the SVM's weights are **not** the same vector — here $(2,0)$ against $(1,0)$:

$$
\mathbf{w}^* = \frac{2(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{d})}{\lVert\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{d}\rVert^2},
\qquad
b^* = -\Big\langle\mathbf{w}^*, \tfrac{\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{d}}{2}\Big\rangle,
\qquad
r = \frac{\lVert\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{d}\rVert}{2}
$$

Checked on $200$ random datasets: $9.185\times10^{-8}$ in $\mathbf{w}$, $4.282\times10^{-8}$ in $b$,
$5.051\times10^{-11}$ in the margin. **The margin is half the distance between the two convex hulls** —
the shortest true statement of what an SVM computes, and one the chapter never makes.

The hull weights are the multipliers renormalised: each class carries dual mass
$\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2/2$, and dividing by it converts one to the other, to $5.780\times10^{-8}$.
:::

## §12.4 Kernels

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| **a kernel** | $k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j) = \langle\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_i),\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_j)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}$ — **12.52** |
| the requirement | $\forall\mathbf{z} : \mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{K}\mathbf{z} \geq 0$ — **12.53** |
| the canonical feature map | $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}) = k(\cdot, \mathbf{x})$ |
| the substitution | replace $\langle\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j\rangle$ in 12.41 by $k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)$ |

| kernel | feature dimension | Gram rank at $N = 50 \dots 800$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| polynomial, degree $2$ | $\binom{D+2}{2} = 6$ | $6, 6, 6, 6, 6$ |
| polynomial, degree $3$ | $10$ | $10, 10, 10, 10, 10$ |
| polynomial, degree $5$ | $21$ | $21, 21, 21, 21, 21$ |
| RBF, $\gamma = 0.5$ | **infinite** | $50, 93, 125, 154, 171$ |
| RBF, $\gamma = 5.0$ | **infinite** | $50, 100, 200, 392, 597$ |

**The polynomial ranks saturate exactly and never move again; the RBF's never level off.** That is
what having no finite feature map looks like from outside, and $\gamma$ controls how fast the space
fills up.

:::caution[Equation 12.53 is a requirement, not a convention]
| function | smallest eigenvalue of $\mathbf{K}$ |
| --- | --- |
| linear | $-2.910\times10^{-14}$ |
| polynomial, degree $3$ | $-5.336\times10^{-13}$ |
| RBF, $\gamma = 0.5$ | $-1.049\times10^{-15}$ |
| $\tanh$ | $\mathbf{-5.544\times10^{1}}$, with $10$ of $120$ negative |

With a negative eigenvalue the dual's quadratic form is not convex, page 1206's duality guarantee does
not apply, and the solver is not solving Equation 12.41.
:::

## §12.5 Numerical solution

| what | equation |

| the dual, standard form | $N$ variables, $2N + 2$ constraint rows — **12.57** |

| --- | --- |
| subgradient set at $t = 1$ | exactly $[-1, 0]$; unique elsewhere |
| subgradient descent, $10^5$ iterations | gap $4.091\times10^{-4}$ — the $O(1/\sqrt{k})$ rate |
| 12.56 solved against the reference | $7.105\times10^{-15}$ |

**With an RBF kernel the primal has infinitely many variables and the dual has $N$.** The dual is not
merely smaller there — it is the only one of the two that exists.

## The chapter in twelve lines

1. **Chapter 12 reasons geometrically, not probabilistically** — closer to Chapter 10's derivation of PCA than to Chapter 9's likelihood — and prints no numbers at all.
2. **A hyperplane is a set, and a set does not fix its parameters.** Distance has no meaning until a scale is chosen, which is what §12.2.1 and §12.2.2 each do differently.
3. **Zero training error picks nothing.** Thousands of separators fit perfectly and disagree by more than ten points of held-out accuracy.
4. **The margin raises the floor rather than the ceiling.** It lands at the 84th percentile every time and is almost never the best available.
5. **Theorem 12.1's two formulations agree to seven millionths of a degree**, and only one of them is convex — which is why nobody solves Equation 12.10.
6. **Slack makes the problem always solvable**, and C is the price of a violation, running backwards from the usual regularisation convention.
7. **Leaving b unregularised is what makes the answer independent of where the origin sits.** Penalising it moves the objective by 10.48 under a shift.
8. **The hinge is a convex upper bound on the zero-one loss that reaches exactly zero**, and that is the property from which everything else follows.
9. **Because the loss reaches zero, most multipliers are zero.** Five of eight examples are deletable and five hundred more are addable, both without changing the answer.
10. **The dual's size depends on N and never on D**, which is the only reason an infinite-dimensional feature map is usable at all.
11. **The same problem read geometrically is: find the two closest points of the two convex hulls, and bisect the segment.** The margin is half its length.
12. **Scaling a feature is pricing it**, and the chapter never mentions standardising — a quarter of a point of accuracy rests on a decision it does not discuss.
