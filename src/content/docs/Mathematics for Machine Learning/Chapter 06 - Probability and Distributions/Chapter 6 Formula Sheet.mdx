## The one-line summary

Two rules generate the chapter, and one family makes them computable:

$$
\underbrace{\text{sum rule}}_{\text{Eq 6.20}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\text{product rule}}_{\text{Eq 6.22}}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\underbrace{\text{Bayes}}_{\text{Eq 6.23}}
\;\xrightarrow[\text{Eq 6.107}]{\text{exponential family}}\;
\underbrace{\text{closed-form inference}}_{\text{conjugacy}}
$$

Everything else is either a named distribution, a summary statistic, or a rule for
transforming one distribution into another.

## The three objects, and the fourth

| Object | Symbol | Lives in | §6.1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| sample space | $\Omega$ | — | the set of outcomes |
| event space | $\mathcal{A}$ | subsets of $\Omega$ | the askable questions; the power set for discrete |
| probability | $P$ | $\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]$ | a measure, $P(\Omega)=1$ |
| target space | $\mathcal{T}$ | — | the quantity of interest |
| random variable | $X$ | $\Omega\to\mathcal{T}$ | **a function**, not a variable |
| law / distribution | $P_X$ | $\mathcal{T}\to[0,1]$ | $P\circ X^{-1}$ |

$$
P_X(S) = P(X\in S) = P\bigl(X^{-1}(S)\bigr) = P\bigl(\{\omega\in\Omega : X(\omega)\in S\}\bigr)
\tag{6.8}
$$

:::caution[The pre-image is the whole point]
$X$ may be non-injective, so several outcomes can share a state. In Example 6.1
two outcomes map to "one dollar coin", so $P(X=1) = 0.21+0.21 = 0.42$, not $0.21$.
Any formula that does not sum over the pre-image gets half the right answer, and
the pmf then fails to sum to $1$ — which is your check.
:::

## §6.2 Discrete and continuous

| # | Result | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| Eq 6.9 | joint $P(X=x_i,Y=y_j) = n_{ij}/N$ | discrete |
| Eq 6.10, 6.11 | marginals $c_i/N$ and $r_j/N$ | discrete |
| Eq 6.12 | probabilities sum to 1 | discrete, finite |
| Eq 6.13 | $P(Y=y_j\mid X=x_i) = n_{ij}/c_i$ — normalises **columns** | — |
| Eq 6.14 | $P(X=x_i\mid Y=y_j) = n_{ij}/r_j$ — normalises **rows** | — |
| Def 6.1 | pdf: $f(\mathbf{x})\geqslant0$ and $\int f = 1$ (Eq 6.15) | **no upper bound on $f$** |
| Eq 6.16 | $P(a\leqslant X\leqslant b) = \int_a^b f$ | continuous |
| Def 6.2 | cdf $F_X(\mathbf{x}) = P(X_1\leqslant x_1,\ldots)$ (Eq 6.17, 6.18) | some cdfs have no pdf |

**Table 6.1**, the nomenclature:

| Type | "point probability" | "interval probability" |
|---|---|---|
| discrete | $P(X=x)$, a **pmf** | not applicable |
| continuous | $p(x)$, a **pdf** | $P(X\leqslant x)$, a **cdf** |

:::danger[A density is not a probability]
Definition 6.1 constrains the **area**, not the height. Measured: the uniform on
$[0.9,1.6]$ has density $1.4286$; a Gaussian with $\sigma=0.01$ peaks at $39.89$;
a uniform of width $10^{-8}$ reaches $10^{8}$ — integral exactly $1$ in every
case. And $P(X=x) = 0$ exactly for continuous $X$: a million uniform samples gave
**zero** exact hits.
:::

## §6.3 The two rules and Bayes

$$
p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\mathbf{y}} p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) \quad\text{or}\quad \int p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}
\tag{6.20}
$$

$$
p(x_i) = \int p(x_1,\ldots,x_D)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\setminus i}
\tag{6.21}
$$

$$
p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x}) = p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})p(\mathbf{y})
\tag{6.22}
$$

$$
\underbrace{p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})}_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\overbrace{p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})}^{\text{likelihood}}\;\overbrace{p(\mathbf{x})}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{p(\mathbf{y})}_{\text{evidence}}}
\tag{6.23}
$$

$$
p(\mathbf{y}) := \int p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x} = \mathbb{E}_X\bigl[p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\bigr]
\tag{6.27}
$$

| Quantity | Normalises over | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| prior $p(x)$ | $x$ | must be **nonzero on every plausible state** |
| likelihood $p(y\mid x)$ | **$y$, not $x$** | never "the likelihood of $y$" |
| evidence $p(y)$ | — (a number) | expected likelihood **under the prior** |
| posterior $p(x\mid y)$ | $x$ | the quantity of interest |

:::danger[Three measured traps]
**The sum rule is the expensive one.** $D$ binary variables give $2^D$ joint
entries — $1.27\times10^{30}$ at $D=100$ — with no known exact polynomial-time
algorithm.

**The base-rate fallacy.** Sensitivity and specificity both $0.99$ at prevalence
$0.001$ gives $P(\text{disease}\mid+) = 0.0902$, not $0.99$ — **$10.98\times$**
overstated. In $100\,000$ people: $99$ true positives against $999$ false.

**A zero prior is permanent.** Measured at exactly `0.0000000000` after $400$
confirming observations, because Equation 6.23 *multiplies*. And the failure is
silent — the remaining states still normalise.
:::

:::danger[Equation 6.44 destroys your answer]
Variance is shift-invariant, so adding a constant must change nothing. Measured on
eight numbers whose variance is $4$:

| offset | Eq 6.43 | Eq 6.44 |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $4.0000000000$ | $4.0000000000$ |
| $10^8$ | $4.0000000000$ | $4.0000000000$ |
| $10^9$ | $4.0000000000$ | **$0.0000000000$** |

Unix timestamps sit at $1.7\times10^9$. Use Equation 6.43 or Welford.
:::

:::danger[Zero covariance is not independence]
Example 6.5: $y = x^2$ with symmetric $x$ gives $\operatorname{Cov} = 0$ while $y$
is a deterministic function of $x$ — total variation distance from independence
$0.406$, and conditioning on $\lvert x\rvert<0.5$ cuts $\mathbb{V}[y]$ from
$1.993$ to $0.005$. Covariance measures **linear** dependence only.
:::

## §6.5 The Gaussian

$$
p(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
\tag{6.62}
$$

$$
p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = (2\pi)^{-\frac D2}\lvert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\rvert^{-\frac12}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right)
\tag{6.63}
$$

| Operation | Result | # |
|---|---|---|
| marginalise | $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_x,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xx})$ — delete rows and columns | 6.68 |
| condition | $\boldsymbol{\mu}_x+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xy}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yy}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_y)$ | 6.66 |
| | $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xx}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xy}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yy}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yx}$ | 6.67 |
| multiply | $\mathbf{C} = (\mathbf{A}^{-1}+\mathbf{B}^{-1})^{-1}$ — precisions **add** | 6.74 |
| | $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{b})$ | 6.75 |
| | $c = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{b},\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})$ | 6.76, 6.77 |
| add independent | $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_x+\boldsymbol{\mu}_y,\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_x+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_y)$ | 6.78 |
| weighted sum | $\mathcal{N}(a\boldsymbol{\mu}_x+b\boldsymbol{\mu}_y,\ a^2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_x+b^2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_y)$ | 6.79 |
| affine map | $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mu},\ \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^\top)$ — **may be singular** | 6.88 |
| reverse map | mean $(\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{y}$ — the pseudo-inverse | 6.91 |
| mix densities | **not Gaussian** | Thm 6.12 |

**Theorem 6.12**, the mixture:

$$
\mathbb{E}[x] = \alpha\mu_1+(1-\alpha)\mu_2
\tag{6.81}
$$

$$
\mathbb{V}[x] = \underbrace{\alpha\sigma_1^2+(1-\alpha)\sigma_2^2}_{\text{within}} + \underbrace{\alpha\mu_1^2+(1-\alpha)\mu_2^2 - \bigl[\alpha\mu_1+(1-\alpha)\mu_2\bigr]^2}_{\text{between}}
\tag{6.82}
$$

an instance of the **law of total variance**,
$\mathbb{V}[x] = \mathbb{E}_Y[\mathbb{V}[x\mid y]] + \mathbb{V}_Y[\mathbb{E}[x\mid y]]$.

**§6.5.4 sampling.** If $\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$ then
$\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\mu}$ has covariance
$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^\top$. Choose $\mathbf{A}$ as the **Cholesky** factor: it
exists because covariances are symmetric positive definite, and it is triangular.

:::caution[Conditioning can only shrink; a mixture is not a Gaussian]
Equation 6.67 subtracts a positive-semidefinite term, so the variance never rises
— measured eigenvalues $[0,\ 0.186,\ 1.847]$ on a 5-D example, and if
$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xy} = \mathbf{0}$ the observation teaches nothing.

And Theorem 6.12's variance is **not** the average of the variances: measured
within $1.0$, between $8.64$, total $9.64$ — the naive answer is $9.64\times$ too
small. A moment-matched Gaussian has excess kurtosis $+0.003$ against the
mixture's $-1.475$, and one mode against two.
:::

## §6.6 Conjugacy and the exponential family

| Distribution | Density | Mean | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bernoulli, Eq 6.92 | $\mu^x(1-\mu)^{1-x}$ | $\mu$ | $\mu(1-\mu)$ |
| Binomial, Eq 6.95 | $\binom Nm\mu^m(1-\mu)^{N-m}$ | $N\mu$ | $N\mu(1-\mu)$ |
| Beta, Eq 6.98 | $\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\mu^{\alpha-1}(1-\mu)^{\beta-1}$ | $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}$ | Eq 6.99 |

**The Beta's four regimes:** $\alpha=\beta=1$ uniform; $\alpha,\beta<1$ bimodal
with endpoint spikes; $\alpha,\beta>1$ unimodal; $\alpha=\beta>1$ symmetric with
mode $\tfrac12$.

**Definition 6.13.** A prior is **conjugate** for a *likelihood* if the posterior
stays in the prior's family. **Table 6.2**:

| Likelihood | Conjugate prior |
|---|---|
| Bernoulli, Binomial | Beta |
| Gaussian (univariate) | Gaussian / inverse Gamma |
| Gaussian (multivariate) | Gaussian / inverse Wishart |
| Multinomial | Dirichlet |

$$
\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta) + h \text{ heads in } N \Longrightarrow \operatorname{Beta}(h+\alpha,\ N-h+\beta)
\tag{6.104d}
$$

**Theorem 6.14 (Fisher–Neyman).** $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ is sufficient for
$\boldsymbol{\theta}$ **iff**

$$
p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}) = h(\mathbf{x})\,g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\bigl(\phi(\mathbf{x})\bigr)
\tag{6.106}
$$

**The exponential family:**

$$
p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}) = h(\mathbf{x})\exp\bigl(\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\phi(\mathbf{x})\rangle - A(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr)
\tag{6.107}
$$

| Piece | Name | Role |
|---|---|---|
| $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ | sufficient statistics | the only route from data to parameters |
| $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ | natural parameters | **unconstrained** |
| $A(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ | log-partition function | normalises; $\partial A/\partial\boldsymbol{\theta} = \mathbb{E}[\phi]$ |
| $h(\mathbf{x})$ | base measure | parameter-free |

| Distribution | $\phi$ | $\theta$ | $A(\theta)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian, Ex 6.13 | $(x, x^2)$ | $\bigl(\mu/\sigma^2,\ -1/(2\sigma^2)\bigr)$ | — |
| Bernoulli, Ex 6.14 | $x$ | $\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}$ | $\log(1+e^\theta)$ |
| Binomial | $m$ | $\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}$ | $N\log(1+e^\theta)$ |
| Beta | $(\log\mu,\ \log(1-\mu))$ | $(\alpha-1,\ \beta-1)$ | $\log B(\alpha,\beta)$ |

$$
\mu = \frac{1}{1+\exp(-\theta)}
\tag{6.118}
$$

the **sigmoid** — which is why logistic regression is a Bernoulli likelihood in
natural parameters. And every member has a conjugate prior by formula:

$$
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}) = h_c(\boldsymbol{\theta})\exp\!\left(\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_1\\\gamma_2\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\theta}\\-A(\boldsymbol{\theta})\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle - A_c(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right)
\tag{6.120}
$$

:::tip[Why conjugacy works, in one line]
Multiplying exponential-family densities **adds natural parameters**. So a Beta
prior times a Binomial likelihood has $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\alpha-1+m,\ \beta-1+N-m)$,
which is $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha+m,\ \beta+N-m)$. Conjugacy is structural, not
lucky — and Pitman, Darmois and Koopman proved (1935–36) that exponential families
are the **only** ones with finite-dimensional sufficient statistics under repeated
sampling.
:::

## §6.7 Change of variables

| Case | Formula | # |
|---|---|---|
| discrete | $P(Y=y) = P\bigl(X=U^{-1}(y)\bigr)$ — **no Jacobian** | 6.125 |
| continuous, univariate | $f(y) = f_x\bigl(U^{-1}(y)\bigr)\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}U^{-1}(y)\right\rvert$ | 6.143 |
| continuous, multivariate | $f(\mathbf{y}) = f_x\bigl(U^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\bigr)\left\lvert\det\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{y}}U^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\right\rvert$ | Thm 6.16, 6.144 |

**Theorem 6.15 (probability integral transform).** For continuous $X$ with a
strictly monotonic cdf, $Y := F_X(X)$ is **uniform**. Run backwards, that is
**inverse-transform sampling**.

:::danger[Two measured traps]
**The Jacobian is not optional.** Dropping $\lvert1/y\rvert$ for $Y=\exp(X)$ gives
a function integrating to $1.6470952$, and *even after renormalising* the worst
pointwise error is $0.5275$ against $0.0047$. It is a **shape** error, so no
constant repairs it.

**The mode is not reparameterisation-invariant.** $X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ has mode
$0$; $Y=\exp(X)$ has mode $\exp(-1) = 0.3679$, not $\exp(0)=1$. Quantiles — and
hence the median — *are* invariant. Only **affine** maps leave the mode alone,
because their Jacobian is constant.
:::

## The eight mistakes this chapter prevents

1. **Reading a density as a probability.** Definition 6.1 bounds the area, not the
   height; $39.89$ is a perfectly ordinary density value.
2. **Forgetting the pre-image.** Equation 6.8 sums over every outcome mapping to a
   state; skip it and Example 6.1's $0.42$ becomes $0.21$.
3. **Transposing a conditional.** Equation 6.13 normalises columns, 6.14 rows.
   Both look right; only the axis sums distinguish them.
4. **Confusing $p(y\mid x)$ with $p(x\mid y)$.** Measured on a medical test:
   $0.99$ against $0.0902$.
5. **Using Equation 6.44 on offset data.** Returns $0$ for a variance of $4$ at an
   offset of $10^9$.
6. **Concluding independence from zero covariance.** Example 6.5 is a
   deterministic relationship with zero covariance.
7. **Averaging component variances for a mixture.** Equation 6.82's second term
   was $90\%$ of the answer.
8. **Dropping the Jacobian in a change of variables**, or trusting a mode across a
   reparameterisation.

## Recall card

- **Two rules generate the chapter.** The sum rule (Eq 6.20) collapses an axis; the product rule (Eq 6.22) factorises a cell. Bayes (Eq 6.23) is those two rearranged.
- **The sum rule is the expensive one:** 2^D terms for D binary variables, 1.27e30 at D = 100, with no known exact polynomial-time algorithm.
- **A random variable is a FUNCTION**, and Eq 6.8 defines its law through the pre-image because that function can collapse outcomes.
- **A density is not a probability.** Def 6.1 bounds the integral, not the height — measured 1.4286, 39.89 and 1e8 with the integral exactly 1 each time. And P(X=x) = 0 exactly for continuous X.
- **The cdf is the only one of the three that is always a probability**, and some cdfs have no pdf.
- **Eq 6.13 normalises columns, Eq 6.14 rows.** Assert the axis sums; a transposed conditional is invisible otherwise.
- **The likelihood is not a distribution in x.** It needs the prior and the evidence to become one.
- **The evidence (Eq 6.27) is the likelihood averaged under the PRIOR**, and it is the hard integral.
- **A zero prior is permanent** — exactly 0.0000000000 after 400 confirming observations, silently.
- **The base-rate fallacy, measured:** P(disease | +) = 0.0902 at prevalence 0.001 with a 99% test, an overstatement of 10.98x.
- **Expectation is linear (Eq 6.34) with no independence needed**; covariance (Eq 6.36) measures only LINEAR dependence, so zero covariance does not imply independence.
- **Eq 6.44 is numerically unsafe:** returns 0 for a variance of 4 once the data is offset by 1e9. Use Eq 6.43.
- **The book's empirical covariance is BIASED (1/N)** and it says so; at N = 2 that is half the true variance.
- **Eq 6.51's V[Ax+b] = A Sigma A-transpose takes the OUTPUT's shape** and can be singular, hence have no density.
- **The Gaussian is closed under everything** — marginalise (6.68), condition (6.66, 6.67), multiply (6.74–6.77), add (6.78), map affinely (6.88) — each in closed form.
- **Conditioning can only shrink the covariance**, and not at all if the cross-covariance is zero.
- **Precisions ADD when Gaussians multiply**, so a Gaussian posterior is always sharper than its prior.
- **A mixture of Gaussians is NOT Gaussian** (Thm 6.12), and its variance has a between-component term that was 90% of the answer in the worked case.
- **Sample a Gaussian with the Cholesky factor:** triangular, and guaranteed to exist for a covariance matrix.
- **Eq 6.107's exponential family has sufficiency built in** — the data reaches the parameters only through phi(x) — and dA/dtheta = E[phi(x)].
- **The Bernoulli's natural parameter is the log-odds, and its inverse is the SIGMOID** (Eq 6.118).
- **Conjugacy is structural:** multiplying exponential-family densities adds natural parameters, so Eq 6.120 generates a conjugate prior for every member.
- **Transforming a continuous variable needs a Jacobian** (Eq 6.143, Thm 6.16); dropping it is a shape error no renormalisation repairs.
- **Theorem 6.15: F_X(X) is uniform** for any continuous X with a strictly monotonic cdf — moments are irrelevant. Backwards, it is inverse-transform sampling.
- **A mode is not reparameterisation-invariant; a quantile is.**
