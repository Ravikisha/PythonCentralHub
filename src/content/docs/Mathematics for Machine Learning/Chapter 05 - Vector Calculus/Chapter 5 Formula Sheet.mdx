Reference, not teaching. Each entry names the book's number, states the result,
gives the **shape** of every object, and names the condition it needs. Read the
concept pages first; come back here when you are working.

## The one-line summary

The whole chapter is one rule applied at four levels of generality:

$$
\underbrace{f'(x)}_{\text{scalar}}
\;\subset\;
\underbrace{\nabla f \in \mathbb{R}^{1\times n}}_{\text{scalar of a vector}}
\;\subset\;
\underbrace{\mathbf{J} \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}_{\text{vector of a vector}}
\;\subset\;
\underbrace{\text{tensors}}_{\text{matrix of a matrix}}
$$

and at every level the chain rule is **the same matrix product**, read left to
right, with the shapes lining up. That is the payoff of §5.2's decision to make
the gradient a row vector.

## The shape rule, which subsumes most of the chapter

**The derivative's shape is the output's shape followed by the input's shape.**

| $f$ maps | $\mathrm{d}f/\mathrm{d}x$ shape | Name |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ | scalar | derivative |
| $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ | $1 \times n$ | gradient (Eq 5.40) |
| $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ | $m \times n$ | Jacobian (Eq 5.56) |
| $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, twice | $n \times n$ | Hessian (Eq 5.147) |
| $\mathbb{R}^{n} \to \mathbb{R}^{m}$, twice | $m \times n \times n$ | a tensor (§5.7 Remark) |
| $\mathbb{R}^{p\times q} \to \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ | $(m\times n)\times(p\times q)$ | a 4-tensor (§5.4) |

If you can state the shape before computing anything, the rest is bookkeeping.
Every dimension error in this chapter is a violation of that one line.

## §5.1 Differentiation of univariate functions

| # | Result | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| Def 5.1 | difference quotient $\dfrac{\delta y}{\delta x} = \dfrac{f(x+\delta x) - f(x)}{\delta x}$ | — |
| Def 5.2 | derivative $f'(x) = \lim\limits_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ | limit must exist |
| — | product: $(uv)' = u'v + uv'$ | both differentiable |
| — | quotient: $\left(\dfrac{u}{v}\right)' = \dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}$ | $v \neq 0$ |
| — | sum: $(u+v)' = u' + v'$ | both differentiable |
| Eq 5.32 | chain rule: $\bigl(g(f(x))\bigr)' = g'(f(x))f'(x)$ | inner differentiable at $x$, outer at $f(x)$ |
| Def 5.3 | Taylor polynomial $T_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\dfrac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k$ | $f$ $n$ times differentiable at $x_0$ |
| Def 5.4 | Taylor series: the same sum to $\infty$ | $f \in C^\infty$; **convergence is a separate question** |

:::caution[The difference quotient has a floor in floating point]
Def 5.2's limit does not exist numerically. Measured on Example 5.14's function:
the central difference improves down to about $h = 10^{-6}$, reaching
$9.8 \times 10^{-12}$, and then gets *worse* — subtracting two nearly equal
numbers destroys digits. A second difference divides by $h^2$ and hits its floor
about a thousand times earlier. This is why
[automatic differentiation](../backpropagation-and-automatic-differentiation/),
which has no $h$ at all, reaches $2.2\times10^{-16}$.
:::

## §5.2 Partial differentiation and gradients

| # | Result | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Def 5.5 | $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} = \lim\limits_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(x_1,\dots,x_i+h,\dots,x_n) - f(\mathbf{x})}{h}$ | scalar |
| Eq 5.40 | $\nabla_\mathbf{x}f = \dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\end{bmatrix}$ | $1 \times n$, a **row** |
| Eq 5.46 | product rule: $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\bigl(f(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{x})\bigr) = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{x}}g + f\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial\mathbf{x}}$ | $1 \times n$ |
| Eq 5.47 | sum rule | $1 \times n$ |
| Eq 5.48 | chain rule: $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}}(g\circ f) = \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial f}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{x}}$ | $1 \times n$ |
| Eq 5.49 | $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\dfrac{\partial x_1}{\partial t} + \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}\dfrac{\partial x_2}{\partial t}$ | scalar |
| Eq 5.50 | $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}(s,t)} = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial(s,t)}$ | $1 \times 2$ |

**Two facts about the gradient, both measurable.** It points in the direction of
steepest ascent, and the steepest rate *equals* its norm — swept over 360
directions, nothing beats $\lVert\nabla f\rVert$ and the exact gradient direction
attains it to $10^{-12}$. And it is perpendicular to the level set: measured
across a whole field, the worst normalised deviation is $10^{-12}$.

## §5.3 Gradients of vector-valued functions

| # | Result | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Eq 5.55 | $\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{f}}{\partial x_i} \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$, a column | $m \times 1$ |
| Eq 5.56 | Jacobian $\mathbf{J} = \nabla_\mathbf{x}\mathbf{f} = \begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial x_n}\end{bmatrix}$ | $m \times n$ |
| Eq 5.58 | $J_{ij} = \dfrac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}$ | — |
| Def 5.6 | $\det\mathbf{J}$ is the local volume scale factor | $n = m$ |
| Eq 5.68 | chain rule for a composition, as a matrix product | $(m\times k)(k\times n)$ |

**Linear maps have constant Jacobians.** For $\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$,
$\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{A}$ everywhere. For a nonlinear map the Jacobian is only
local — measured on a fold-free patch, the ratio of a small square's image area to
$\lvert\det\mathbf{J}\rvert$ converges to $1$ at first order in the patch size.

:::danger[Where det J changes sign, area is not what you measured]
A shoelace area is *signed*. If the patch straddles the curve where
$\det\mathbf{J} = 0$ the polygon folds over itself and the formula returns the
difference of two signed pieces, not their sum. Measured on a map with
$\det\mathbf{J} = 1 - 1.2uv$: a square at $(0.8, 0.6)$ straddles the fold at
$uv = 0.8333$ and its "area" came out $0.04$ against a $\lvert\det\mathbf{J}\rvert$
of $0.424$. Rebased away from the fold, the agreement is clean.
:::

## §5.4 Gradients of matrices

| # | Result | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| — | $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{A}}$ for $\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^m$, $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}$ | $m \times (p \times q)$ |
| Eq 5.85 | $\mathbf{f} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$: $\dfrac{\partial f_i}{\partial A_{i,:}} = \mathbf{x}^\top$, and $\mathbf{0}$ for every other row | $1 \times n$ per slice |
| — | $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{K}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R}}$ for $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}$ | $(n\times n)\times(m\times n)$ |

**Two ways to organise the same numbers.** Either keep the tensor shape, or
flatten the matrix to a $pq$-vector and get an ordinary Jacobian. The book does
both; the flattened form is what a library computes, and the tensor form is what
makes the *shapes* comprehensible.

## §5.5 Useful identities

With the book's Equations 5.99 to 5.108. Every one of these is verified
numerically on the [identities page](../useful-identities-for-computing-gradients/).

| # | Identity |
|---|---|
| Eq 5.99 | $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})^\top = \left(\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\right)^\top$ |
| Eq 5.100 | $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})\bigr) = \mathrm{tr}\left(\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\right)$ |
| Eq 5.101 | $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\det\bigl(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})\bigr) = \det\bigl(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})\bigr)\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})^{-1}\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\right)$ |
| Eq 5.102 | $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})^{-1} = -\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})^{-1}\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ |
| Eq 5.103 | $\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{X}^{-1}\mathbf{b}}{\partial\mathbf{X}} = -(\mathbf{X}^{-1})^\top\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top(\mathbf{X}^{-1})^\top$ |
| Eq 5.104 | $\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{a}}{\partial\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{a}^\top$ |
| Eq 5.105 | $\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}}{\partial\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top$ |
| Eq 5.106 | $\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}}{\partial\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x}^\top(\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{B}^\top)$ |
| Eq 5.107 | $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s})^\top\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}) = -2(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s})^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}$ |
| Eq 5.108 | the same for symmetric $\mathbf{W}$ |

:::danger[Equation 5.106 is the one people get wrong]
The derivative of a quadratic form is $\mathbf{x}^\top(\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{B}^\top)$,
**not** $2\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{B}$. They agree only when $\mathbf{B}$ is
symmetric. Measured on a deliberately non-symmetric matrix, the shortcut is
**62.5% relative** wrong — a plausible answer with the right shape. Equation
5.108's symmetric shortcut is worse still if you apply it unconditionally: it came
out **1.20 relative (120%)** in one test and $2.9\times10^{-1}$ in another.
:::

## §5.6 Backpropagation and automatic differentiation

| # | Result |
|---|---|
| Eq 5.109 | the example function $f(x) = \sqrt{x^2+\exp(x^2)} + \cos(x^2+\exp(x^2))$ |
| Eq 5.110 | its explicit derivative — 11 operations, and it recomputes $x^2$ and $\exp(x^2)$ |
| Eq 5.111 | $\mathbf{f}_0 = \mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{f}_i = \sigma_i(\mathbf{A}_{i-1}\mathbf{f}_{i-1} + \mathbf{b}_{i-1})$ |
| Eq 5.115–5.118 | the cascade: $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_i}$ extends the previous product by one factor |
| Eq 5.119–5.121 | forward and reverse mode as two **bracketings** of one product of Jacobians |
| Eq 5.123–5.128 | Example 5.14's forward pass, six intermediates |
| Eq 5.129–5.134 | its elementary local derivatives |
| Eq 5.135–5.142 | its reverse sweep |
| Eq 5.143–5.145 | the general algorithm; **Eq 5.145's sum** is over all children of a node |

**The costs, measured.** Reverse mode costs one sweep per *output*, forward mode
one per *input*. On Example 5.14: 6 forward operations against 16 in the reverse
sweep — a factor of $2.67$, a constant rather than a blow-up. With a scalar loss
the crossover is at $n = 3$, so for a network with millions of parameters reverse
mode is not a preference but the only option. And AD's error was
$2.2\times10^{-16}$ against the best of 200 central differences at
$9.8\times10^{-12}$.

:::danger[A node with two children needs a sum]
Equation 5.145 sums over children, and Example 5.14's node $a$ is the only one
with two — so Equation 5.137 is the only adjoint in the whole example that is a
sum rather than a single product, and therefore the only one easy to forget.
Dropping its second term leaves the gradient the right sign and order of
magnitude but **9.5% to 47.8% wrong**, worst for *small* $x$, which inverts the
usual debugging instinct.
:::

## §5.7 Higher-order derivatives

| # | Result | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| — | $\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x} = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)$: the operator nearest $f$ acts first | — |
| Eq 5.146 | $\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y} = \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}$ | **twice continuously** differentiable |
| Eq 5.147 | $H = \begin{bmatrix}\partial^2_{xx}f & \partial^2_{xy}f \\ \partial^2_{xy}f & \partial^2_{yy}f\end{bmatrix}$, symmetric, written $\nabla^2_{x,y}f$ | $n \times n$ in general |
| — | curvature along a unit $\mathbf{d}$ is $\mathbf{d}^\top H\mathbf{d}$, bounded by the eigenvalues and attaining both | $H$ symmetric |
| Remark | a vector field's Hessian is an $(m\times n\times n)$ tensor | — |

**The second-order test has four outcomes.** All eigenvalues positive: minimum.
All negative: maximum. Mixed: saddle. **Some zero: the test says nothing** — and
$x_1^2+x_2^4$, $x_1^2+x_2^3$ and $x_1^2-x_2^4$ all share the Hessian
$\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ at the origin while being, respectively, a
strict minimum and two non-optima.

:::danger[Never test an eigenvalue against zero]
A central second difference has a truncation floor of exactly $2h^2$, and it is
**positive** — measured from $2.0\times10^{-4}$ at $h = 10^{-2}$ down to
$2.0\times10^{-14}$ at $h=10^{-7}$, reporting a singular Hessian as positive
definite at every step size. Compare against a tolerance scaled to the entries.
:::

:::caution[A symmetric stencil cannot verify Equation 5.146]
The five-point mixed stencil computes both orders from the same four corner
values, so its gap is `0.0e+00` for every function — including the counterexample
$xy(x^2-y^2)/(x^2+y^2)$, whose two iterated limits at the origin are $-1$ and
$+1$, a gap of exactly $2$ at every step size.
:::

## §5.8 Linearization and multivariate Taylor series

| # | Result | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Eq 5.148 | $f(\mathbf{x}) \approx f(\mathbf{x}_0) + (\nabla_\mathbf{x}f)(\mathbf{x}_0)(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)$ | $(1\times D)(D\times 1)$ |
| Def 5.7 / Eq 5.151 | $f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{D^k_\mathbf{x}f(\mathbf{x}_0)}{k!}\boldsymbol{\delta}^k$, $\boldsymbol{\delta} := \mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0$ | — |
| Def 5.8 / Eq 5.152 | $T_n(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\dfrac{D^k_\mathbf{x}f(\mathbf{x}_0)}{k!}\boldsymbol{\delta}^k$ | — |
| Eq 5.153 | $\boldsymbol{\delta}^2 = \boldsymbol{\delta}\otimes\boldsymbol{\delta} = \boldsymbol{\delta}\boldsymbol{\delta}^\top$, entries $\delta_i\delta_j$ | $D\times D$ |
| Eq 5.154 | $\boldsymbol{\delta}^3$, entries $\delta_i\delta_j\delta_k$ | $D\times D\times D$ |
| Eq 5.155 | $D^k f\,\boldsymbol{\delta}^k = \sum_{i_1}\cdots\sum_{i_k} D^kf[i_1..i_k]\delta_{i_1}\cdots\delta_{i_k}$ | scalar |
| Eq 5.156 | $k=0$: $f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ | scalar |
| Eq 5.157 | $k=1$: $\nabla_\mathbf{x}f(\mathbf{x}_0)\boldsymbol{\delta}$ — `einsum("i,i", Df1, d)` | scalar |
| Eq 5.158 | $k=2$: $\mathrm{tr}(H\boldsymbol{\delta}\boldsymbol{\delta}^\top) = \boldsymbol{\delta}^\top H\boldsymbol{\delta}$ | scalar |
| Eq 5.159 | $= \sum_i\sum_j H_{ij}\delta_i\delta_j$ — `einsum("ij,i,j", Df2, d, d)` | scalar |
| Eq 5.160 | $k=3$ — `einsum("ijk,i,j,k", Df3, d, d, d)` | scalar |

**The measured facts.** The order-$n$ error scales like $r^{n+1}$: fitted slopes
$1.067$, $2.005$, $3.019$ against the predicted $1$, $2$, $3$. But that is a
statement about $r \to 0$ — on a fixed window a higher-order model can be worse,
and at $r = 3.2$ a quadratic model's error of $7.77$ was **five times** the
constant model's $1.40$.

**The cost.** $D^k$ entries at order $k$; $\binom{D+k-1}{k}$ distinct after
Equation 5.146's symmetry. At $D = 10^6$: $5.0\times10^{11}$ at $k = 2$ (4 TB in
float32) and $1.7\times10^{17}$ at $k = 3$. Symmetry halves the constant and does
nothing to the exponent.

:::caution[The cross term keeps a factor of 2]
Equation 5.159's double sum counts $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$ separately. Expanding
$\tfrac12\boldsymbol{\delta}^\top H\boldsymbol{\delta}$ for Example 5.15 gives
$(x-1)^2 + 2(x-1)(y-2) + 6(y-2)^2$ — a $2$ on the middle term and no $\tfrac12$
anywhere. Wrong by a factor of two on exactly one term is the hardest kind of
error to spot.
:::

## The eight mistakes this chapter is designed to prevent

1. **Writing the gradient as a column.** Equation 5.40 makes it a row so the chain
   rule is a left-to-right matrix product. Get this wrong and every subsequent
   shape needs a transpose you will forget.
2. **Using $2\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{B}$ for a quadratic form's derivative.** It is
   $\mathbf{x}^\top(\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}^\top)$ unless $\mathbf{B}$ is symmetric.
   Measured cost of the shortcut on a non-symmetric matrix: 62.5%.
3. **Forgetting Equation 5.145's sum** at a node with more than one child. Costs
   9.5% to 47.8% on Example 5.14, and looks plausible.
4. **Testing a Hessian eigenvalue against zero.** The differencing floor is
   $+2h^2$, so a singular Hessian reads as positive definite at every $h$.
5. **Treating a zero eigenvalue as "no optimum".** It means *ask a higher
   derivative*: three functions share one singular Hessian and have three
   different answers.
6. **Expecting Newton's method to find a minimum.** It solves
   $\nabla f = \mathbf{0}$, with no preference. On $x_1^2 - x_2^2$ one step lands
   exactly on the saddle and reports success.
7. **Reading "higher order is better" as a statement about your window.** It is a
   statement about $r \to 0$; at $r = 3.2$ the quadratic model was five times
   worse than the constant one.
8. **Dropping the second path in a total derivative** when a variable appears
   more than once. Exercise 5.9's truncated gradient is 102% wrong and points the
   opposite way.

## Every NumPy call in the chapter

| Task | Call |
|---|---|
| numerical gradient | `(f(x+h) - f(x-h)) / (2*h)`, per component |
| Jacobian, column by column | same, stacked with `np.column_stack` |
| local volume factor | `np.linalg.det(J)` |
| contract Eq 5.157 | `np.einsum("i,i", Df1, d)` |
| contract Eq 5.159 | `np.einsum("ij,i,j", Df2, d, d)` |
| contract Eq 5.160 | `np.einsum("ijk,i,j,k", Df3, d, d, d)` |
| quadratic form, cheaply | `d @ H @ d`, never `np.trace(H @ np.outer(d, d))` |
| Hessian eigenvalues | `np.linalg.eigvalsh(H)` — symmetric only, and it does not check |
| is $H$ positive definite | `np.linalg.cholesky(H)` in a `try`, about half the cost |

## Recall card

- **The shape rule subsumes the chapter:** the derivative's shape is the output's shape followed by the input's. Scalar of a vector is 1 by n, vector of a vector is m by n, matrix of a vector is m by n by n.
- **Equation 5.40 makes the gradient a ROW**, so the chain rule is a left-to-right matrix product and Equation 5.148 needs no transpose.
- **Equation 5.32's chain rule is the only rule in the chapter.** §5.2, §5.3, §5.6 and §5.8 are all it, at increasing levels of generality.
- **Equation 5.56's Jacobian is m by n**, and det J is the local volume scale factor — local only, and meaningless where it changes sign.
- **Equation 5.106: the derivative of a quadratic form is x-transpose (B + B-transpose)**, not 2 x-transpose B. Measured cost of the shortcut on a non-symmetric B: 62.5 percent.
- **Equation 5.145 sums over a node's children.** Example 5.14's node a is the only one with two, and dropping the second term costs 9.5 to 47.8 percent, worst for small x.
- **Reverse mode is one sweep per OUTPUT, forward mode one per INPUT.** Crossover at n = 3 for a scalar loss, and the reverse sweep costs 2.67 times the forward pass on Example 5.14 — a constant, not a blow-up.
- **AD has no step size:** 2.2e-16 against the best of 200 central differences at 9.8e-12.
- **Equation 5.146 needs its hypothesis** — twice CONTINUOUSLY differentiable. The standard counterexample's two iterated limits at the origin are -1 and +1, gap exactly 2.
- **Equation 5.147's Hessian is symmetric**, so §4.2 applies, and the curvature along a unit d is d-transpose H d, squeezed between the eigenvalues and attaining both.
- **The second-order test has FOUR outcomes**, and the fourth — some eigenvalue zero — means ask a higher derivative. Three functions share [[2,0],[0,0]] and have three different answers.
- **Never test an eigenvalue against zero:** the second-difference floor is exactly 2h^2 and it is positive.
- **Newton's method solves grad f = 0**, not "find a minimum". 8 steps against gradient descent's 426 on a convex test, and exactly onto the saddle on x1^2 - x2^2.
- **The condition number is the price gradient descent pays:** 18, 94, 939, 9384 steps for kappa of 2, 10, 100, 1000.
- **Definition 5.7's delta-to-the-k is a k-fold OUTER product**, and the order-k tensor has D^k entries — 1e12 at a million parameters, which is where practice stops.
- **Taylor error goes like r to the (n+1) as r goes to zero**, measured 1.067, 2.005, 3.019 — and on a fixed window a higher order can be five times worse.
