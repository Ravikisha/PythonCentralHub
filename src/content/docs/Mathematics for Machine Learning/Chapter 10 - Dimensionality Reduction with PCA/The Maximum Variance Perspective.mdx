Page 1001 fixed the code length $M$ and left the basis open. §10.2 closes it, from one assumption:

> If we interpret information content in the data as how *"space filling"* the dataset is, then we can
> describe the information contained in the data by looking at the **spread** of the data.

That is a modelling choice, not a theorem — and it is worth noticing that it is one, because §10.3 will
minimise something apparently different and arrive at exactly the same $\mathbf{B}$.

## What you'll learn

- Equations 10.7–10.9b: how *"maximise the variance of the code"* collapses to $\mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_1$ — one Chapter 6 identity and one line of algebra. Measured, all three readings agree to $8.5\times10^{-14}$.
- Why the constraint $\lVert\mathbf{b}_1\rVert^2 = 1$ is not cosmetic: without it the objective has no maximum at all.
- Equations 10.11–10.14: the Lagrangian, and the moment $\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_1 = \lambda_1\mathbf{b}_1$ appears — *"the Lagrange multiplier plays the role of the corresponding eigenvalue."*
- **Equation 10.15, $V_1 = \lambda_1$** — measured for the first six components, largest gap $8.5\times10^{-14}$. The variance you keep *is* the eigenvalue; there is nothing else to compute.
- Equation 10.17's deflation and Equation 10.21's claim that $\mathbf{S}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{S}}$ share eigenvectors — verified by deflating four times and recovering each $\mathbf{b}_m$ to under $2.1\times10^{-6}$ degrees.
- Equations 10.24 and 10.25: $V_M = \sum_{m\le M}\lambda_m$ and $J_M = \sum_{j>M}\lambda_j$, and the fact that the *measured* reconstruction error matches $J_M$ to $2.8\times10^{-13}$ — a result §10.3 will spend a whole section deriving.
- Why a random search is hopeless in $64$ dimensions: $200{,}000$ random unit vectors reach $37.18\%$ of $\lambda_1$, while power iteration reaches it in $500$ cheap steps.

## Intuition: the objective is a function on a circle

Strip §10.2 to two dimensions and the whole argument fits in one picture. A unit vector in the plane is
just an angle. Feed it to $\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}$ and you get a number. Sweep the angle and
you get a curve — and the curve has a maximum somewhere.

Two things the picture makes obvious that the algebra hides:

**The curve is smooth and has exactly two stationary points** on the half-circle — one maximum, one
minimum. Those are the two eigenvectors. In $D$ dimensions there are $D$ of them, which is why one
eigendecomposition answers the question for every $M$ at once.

**You cannot find the peak by sampling.** In two dimensions you could. In $64$ dimensions the surface of
the unit sphere is overwhelmingly far from any particular direction: measured below, $200{,}000$ random
unit vectors get to $37.18\%$ of the maximum.

## §10.2.1 From "spread" to an eigenvalue problem

The variance of the first coordinate of the code, using the i.i.d. assumption:

$$
V_1 := \mathbb{V}[z_1] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} z_{1n}^2, \qquad z_{1n} = \mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{x}_n
\qquad \text{(10.7), (10.8)}
$$

Substituting and using the symmetry of the dot product, $\mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{x}_n = \mathbf{x}_n^\top\mathbf{b}_1$:

$$
V_1 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{x}_n\mathbf{x}_n^\top\mathbf{b}_1
= \mathbf{b}_1^\top\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{x}_n\mathbf{x}_n^\top\right)\!\mathbf{b}_1
= \mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_1 \qquad \text{(10.9a), (10.9b)}
$$

The only move is pulling $\mathbf{b}_1$ outside the sum, which is legal because it does not depend on $n$.
Measured on the digit-"8" data, three routes to the same number:

| route | value |
| --- | --- |
| $\frac{1}{N}\sum_n z_{1n}^2$, Equation 10.7 | $151.568412$ |
| $\mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_1$, Equation 10.9b | $151.568412$ |
| `np.var` of the code | $151.568412$ |
| $\lambda_1$, Equation 10.15 | $151.568412$ |
| largest gap between any two | $8.5\times10^{-14}$ |

(The code's mean comes out at $2.5\times10^{-16}$, so Equation 10.7's *"mean of the squares"* really is
the variance, as the book's centred-data remark promises.)

:::note[Why Equation 10.6 lets the whole section assume mean zero]
$\mathbb{V}_z[\mathbf{z}] = \mathbb{V}_x[\mathbf{B}^\top(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)]
= \mathbb{V}_x[\mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{B}^\top\boldsymbol\mu]
= \mathbb{V}_x[\mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{x}]$ — a variance is blind to a shift, so subtracting the mean
changes nothing about the objective.

This is *"without loss of generality"* in the strict sense and it is worth separating from page 1001's
measurement. Centring does not change what the objective *means*; it changes what
$\frac{1}{N}\sum\mathbf{x}_n\mathbf{x}_n^\top$ *is*. If you centre, Equation 10.1 computes the covariance
and maximising $\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}$ maximises the variance. If you don't, it computes
something else, and you maximise that instead — which is how the leading direction ended up
$0.2851^\circ$ from the mean image.
:::

### The constraint is load-bearing

*"Arbitrarily increasing the magnitude of the vector $\mathbf{b}_1$ increases $V_1$"* — doubling
$\mathbf{b}_1$ quadruples $\mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_1$. So the unconstrained problem has a
supremum of $+\infty$ and no maximiser. Restricting to $\lVert\mathbf{b}_1\rVert^2 = 1$ gives

$$
\max_{\mathbf{b}_1}\ \mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_1
\quad\text{subject to}\quad \lVert\mathbf{b}_1\rVert^2 = 1 \qquad \text{(10.10)}
$$

and Chapter 7's Lagrangian

$$
\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{b}_1, \lambda) = \mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_1
+ \lambda_1\!\left(1 - \mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{b}_1\right) \qquad \text{(10.11)}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial\mathfrak{L}}{\partial\mathbf{b}_1} = 2\mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{S} - 2\lambda_1\mathbf{b}_1^\top,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\mathfrak{L}}{\partial\lambda_1} = 1 - \mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{b}_1
\qquad \text{(10.12)}
$$

Setting both to $\mathbf{0}$:

$$
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_1 = \lambda_1\mathbf{b}_1, \qquad \mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{b}_1 = 1
\qquad \text{(10.13), (10.14)}
$$

*"By comparing this with the definition of an eigenvalue decomposition, we see that $\mathbf{b}_1$ is an
eigenvector of the data covariance matrix $\mathbf{S}$, and the Lagrange multiplier $\lambda_1$ plays the
role of the corresponding eigenvalue."* Measured — the gradient $2\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b} - 2\lambda_1\mathbf{b}$
at three unit vectors:

| unit vector, with $\lambda = \lambda_1$ | $\lVert$gradient$\rVert$ |
| --- | --- |
| $\mathbf{b}_1$ | $\mathbf{0.000000}$ |
| $\mathbf{b}_2$ | $127.709219$ |
| a random direction (seed $0$) | $285.800195$ |

### Equation 10.15, which is the whole point

Because $\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_1 = \lambda_1\mathbf{b}_1$ and $\mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{b}_1 = 1$:

$$
V_1 = \mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_1 = \lambda_1\mathbf{b}_1^\top\mathbf{b}_1 = \lambda_1
\qquad \text{(10.15)}
$$

| $m$ | $\mathbf{b}_m^\top\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_m$ | $\lambda_m$ | gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | $151.56841162$ | $151.56841162$ | $8.5\times10^{-14}$ |
| $2$ | $87.71380214$ | $87.71380214$ | $5.7\times10^{-14}$ |
| $3$ | $75.28494722$ | $75.28494722$ | $1.4\times10^{-14}$ |
| $4$ | $54.05513090$ | $54.05513090$ | $7.1\times10^{-15}$ |
| $5$ | $52.41321109$ | $52.41321109$ | $3.6\times10^{-14}$ |
| $6$ | $42.57742529$ | $42.57742529$ | $0.0$ |

**The eigendecomposition does not just tell you where to look — it tells you what you will find there.**
No projection needs to be formed to know how much variance a component keeps. The book's margin note
adds the name: $\sqrt{\lambda_1}$ is the *loading* of $\mathbf{b}_1$, the standard deviation accounted
for by $\mathrm{span}[\mathbf{b}_1]$.

:::tip[Why nobody searches for the maximum, and what they do instead]
On the digit-"8" covariance, $200{,}000$ random unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{64}$:

| method | best $\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}$ found | share of $\lambda_1$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $200{,}000$ random unit vectors | $56.351776$ | $37.18\%$ |
| power iteration, $500$ steps | $151.568412$ | $100\%$ |

The second lands $1.2\times10^{-6}$ degrees from $\mathbf{b}_1$ using nothing but repeated
$\mathbf{b} \leftarrow \mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}/\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}\rVert$ — which is §10.4.2's
Equation 10.52, and the original PageRank algorithm.
:::

## §10.2.2 The second component, and why there is nothing new to do

For $\mathbf{b}_m$ with $m > 1$, the book's device is to remove what the earlier components already
explain:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{X}} := \mathbf{X} - \sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\mathbf{b}_i\mathbf{b}_i^\top\mathbf{X}
= \mathbf{X} - \mathbf{B}_{m-1}\mathbf{X} \qquad \text{(10.17)}
$$

then maximise $V_m = \mathbf{b}_m^\top\hat{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{b}_m$ against the deflated covariance,
which is the §10.2.1 problem again. Run literally — deflate, re-decompose, take the top eigenvector,
repeat:

| $m$ | top eigenvalue of $\hat{\mathbf{S}}$ | $\lambda_m$ of $\mathbf{S}$ | angle to $\mathbf{b}_m$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | $151.568412$ | $151.568412$ | $1.7\times10^{-6}$ deg |
| $2$ | $87.713802$ | $87.713802$ | $2.1\times10^{-6}$ deg |
| $3$ | $75.284947$ | $75.284947$ | $1.2\times10^{-6}$ deg |
| $4$ | $54.055131$ | $54.055131$ | $0.0$ deg |
| $5$ | $52.413211$ | $52.413211$ | $0.0$ deg |

The book proves this must happen. Split the eigenvectors of $\mathbf{S}$ by whether they have been used:

$$
\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\mathbf{b}_i = \mathbf{b}_i \ \ \text{if } i < m,
\qquad
\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\mathbf{b}_i = \mathbf{0} \ \ \text{if } i \geq m \qquad \text{(10.20)}
$$

Expanding $\hat{\mathbf{S}} = \mathbf{S} - \mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}_{m-1} - \mathbf{B}_{m-1}\mathbf{S} + \mathbf{B}_{m-1}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}_{m-1}$
and applying each case gives

$$
\hat{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{b}_m = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_m = \lambda_m\mathbf{b}_m \qquad \text{(10.21)}
$$

$$
\hat{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{b}_i = \mathbf{0} = 0\,\mathbf{b}_i \quad\text{for } i < m \qquad \text{(10.22)}
$$

<Figure
  src="/images/mml/the-maximum-variance-perspective/deflation-moves-the-spectrum"
  alt="Left, four descending eigenvalue curves; each successive curve starts one step lower, so the four lines are the same sequence shifted left by zero, one, two and three places. Right, a log-scale bar chart of six values: the first three sit at about 1e-14 and the last three at about 50."
  title="Deflating removes one eigenvalue and leaves every other one alone"
  caption="Left: after removing m components, the deflated spectrum is the original one with its first m entries deleted — the curves overlay exactly, measured to 7.8e-14. Right: the three used-up directions become null vectors of S-hat, with residual norms 1.0e-14, 5.9e-15 and 7.1e-15 against roughly 50 for the untouched ones."
/>

:::caution[The deflation is a proof, not a procedure]
Every eigenvector of $\mathbf{S}$ is still an eigenvector of $\hat{\mathbf{S}}$ — the used ones with
eigenvalue $0$, the rest with the eigenvalue they had. So the $m$-th round of the sequential search
returns the $m$-th eigenvector of the **original** $\mathbf{S}$, which one call to `np.linalg.eigh`
already handed you.

Implementing Equation 10.17 literally costs $m$ eigendecompositions to obtain what one produces. Its job
in the book is to justify the greedy argument — that taking the top $M$ eigenvectors is optimal, and not
merely a sequence of locally optimal choices.
:::

With Equation 10.21 in hand, $V_m = \mathbf{b}_m^\top\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_m = \lambda_m$ for every $m$
(Equation 10.23), and summing:

$$
V_M = \sum_{m=1}^{M}\lambda_m, \qquad
J_M := \sum_{j=M+1}^{D}\lambda_j = V_D - V_M \qquad \text{(10.24), (10.25)}
$$

**The last column was never mentioned in §10.2.** It is the average squared reconstruction error, formed
by actually building $\tilde{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{X}$ and measuring — and across
every $M$ from $1$ to $63$ it agrees with $J_M$ to $2.8\times10^{-13}$.

That equality is §10.3's entire result, arrived at here for free. §10.2 maximised the variance kept;
the error paid came out as the leftover eigenvalues without anyone asking. §10.3 turns the observation
into a derivation, and Equation 10.44 will state it.

| variance retained | components needed | share of the $64$ dimensions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $50\%$ | $M = 5$ | $7.8\%$ |
| $80\%$ | $M = 12$ | $18.8\%$ |
| $90\%$ | $M = 18$ | $28.1\%$ |
| $95\%$ | $M = 25$ | $39.1\%$ |
| $99\%$ | $M = 36$ | $56.2\%$ |

:::note[Reading the flat part of the spectrum honestly]
The book's Example 10.2 says *"only a few of them have a value that differs significantly from 0"*, and
at MNIST's $D = 784$ that is a fair description. At $D = 64$ it is less dramatic: $\lambda_1$ is
$20.45\%$ of the total and $\lambda_1/\lambda_2 = 1.73$, so there is no single dominant direction. Half
the variance still costs $5$ components and $99\%$ costs $36$.

Two lessons that survive both scales. First, **the twelve exact zeros are free** — they are directions on
which the data has no spread at all, and discarding them loses literally nothing. Second, everything
after that is a judgement call about how much loss is acceptable, and §10.2 gives you the exact price of
every option in one sorted list.
:::

## Recall card

- **Section 10.2 assumes that information is spread**, and derives PCA by maximising the variance of the code. That is a modelling choice, and Section 10.3 reaches the same answer from a different one.
- **Equation 10.9b turns the objective into b-transpose S b** in one line, using only the symmetry of the dot product. Measured three ways on the digit-8 data, all agreeing to 8.5e-14.
- **The unit-norm constraint is load-bearing:** doubling b quadruples the objective, so without it there is no maximum at all.
- **The Lagrangian's stationarity condition is the eigenvalue equation.** Measured, the gradient is exactly zero at the leading eigenvector and 127.709219 at the second.
- **Equation 10.15: the variance kept equals the eigenvalue.** So one sorted eigenvalue list prices every possible choice of M, with no projection formed.
- **Two hundred thousand random unit vectors in 64 dimensions reach 37.18 percent of the largest eigenvalue.** Power iteration reaches it exactly in 500 steps.
- **Equation 10.17's deflation is a proof device, not an algorithm.** S and the deflated S-hat share eigenvectors; the used-up ones simply acquire eigenvalue zero, measured at about 1e-14.
- **So the greedy sequential choice is genuinely optimal**, not merely locally optimal — the m-th round returns the m-th eigenvector of the original S.
- **Equations 10.24 and 10.25 split the total variance** into what is kept and what is lost, and the two always sum to the trace.
- **The lost part equals the measured reconstruction error, to 2.8e-13**, even though Section 10.2 never mentioned reconstruction. Section 10.3 derives that.
- **On 174 images of the digit eight, half the variance costs 5 components and ninety-nine percent costs 36.** Twelve eigenvalues are machine zero — the dead border pixels, free to discard.
