Six sections of PCA and not one probability. §10.7 adds one, and the book is direct about why:

> On the one hand, this approach may be appealing as it allows us to sidestep all the mathematical
> difficulties that come with probability theory, but on the other hand, a probabilistic model would
> offer us more flexibility and useful insights.

The payoff list is eight items long, and it is the same list Chapter 9 earned by going from §9.2 to
§9.3: a likelihood, model comparison by marginal likelihood, a **generative** model, missing data by
Bayes' theorem, and a notion of novelty.

## What you'll learn

- Equations 10.63–10.66: the generative process, and what it means that the arrow points **from** $\mathbf{z}$ **to** $\mathbf{x}$.
- Equations 10.69–10.70b: $p(\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu,\ \mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top + \sigma^2\mathbf{I})$ — checked by $400{,}000$ ancestral samples, agreeing to $2.1\times10^{-3}$ relative, the Monte-Carlo floor.
- Equation 10.71: the cross-covariance $\mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}]$ is exactly $\mathbf{B}$.
- Equations 10.73–10.75: the posterior over the code. **$\mathbf{C}$ does not depend on $\mathbf{x}$** — measured, exactly $0$ change across $200$ observations — which is the same structural fact page 907 measured for $\mathbf{S}_N$.
- A closed form the book does not give: $\mathbf{C} = \sigma^2(\mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{B} + \sigma^2\mathbf{I})^{-1}$, which for the ML $\mathbf{B}$ is **diagonal with entries $\sigma^2/\lambda_m$** — measured to $9.7\times10^{-16}$.
- Equations 10.77–10.79: $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}}$ is page 1004's $J_M$ divided by $D-M$, matched to eight decimals.
- **PPCA is PCA with shrinkage.** The reconstruction is the PCA projection scaled by $(\lambda_m-\sigma^2)/\lambda_m$ on each axis — measured to six decimals at four noise levels — and converges to PCA as $\sigma\to0$.

## Intuition: PCA describes the data, PPCA claims to have made it

Everything before this page is a statement about a dataset you already have: here is the subspace that
keeps the most variance. Nothing in it says what a *new* image would look like, or how surprised you
should be by one.

PPCA writes down a story instead. Draw $\mathbf{z}$ from a standard Gaussian; push it through an affine
map; add noise. That story assigns a probability to every point in $\mathbb{R}^D$, which is what buys
the eight items on the list — and it also commits you to claims that can be wrong.

## §10.7.1 The generative process

$$
\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{z} + \boldsymbol\mu + \boldsymbol\epsilon \in \mathbb{R}^D,
\qquad \boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I}) \qquad \text{(10.63)}
$$

$$
\mathbf{z}_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}),
\qquad
\mathbf{x}_n \mid \mathbf{z}_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}_n + \boldsymbol\mu, \sigma^2\mathbf{I})
\qquad \text{(10.65), (10.66)}
$$

This is *ancestral sampling*: draw the parent, then the child.

:::note[The direction of the arrow is a claim, not a convention]
> The arrow points from $\mathbf{z}$ to $\mathbf{x}$, which means that the PPCA model assumes a
> **lower-dimensional latent cause** $\mathbf{z}$ for high-dimensional observations $\mathbf{x}$.

Compare page 807's directed graphical models. PCA computed $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{x}$ — a
function of the data, pointing the other way. PPCA asserts the reverse and then *derives*
$\mathbf{z}$-given-$\mathbf{x}$ by Bayes' theorem, *"to 'invert' the arrow implicitly"*.

The two happen to give almost the same answer here. They would not for a nonlinear map, and the whole
of variational autoencoders lives in that gap.
:::

## §10.7.2 The likelihood, and why it needs the integral

The conditional $p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})$ cannot be maximised over parameters, because it still
mentions $\mathbf{z}$. Integrating it out:

$$
p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol\mu,\sigma^2)
= \int \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}+\boldsymbol\mu,\sigma^2\mathbf{I})\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}
\qquad \text{(10.68b)}
$$

$$
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}] = \boldsymbol\mu,
\qquad
\mathbb{V}[\mathbf{x}] = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top + \sigma^2\mathbf{I} \qquad \text{(10.69), (10.70b)}
$$

Verified by running Equations 10.65 and 10.66 forward $400{,}000$ times with $M = 5$, $\sigma^2 = 4$:

| | value |
| --- | --- |
| $\lVert$empirical mean $-\ \boldsymbol\mu\rVert$ | $0.037050$ |
| $\max\lvert$empirical covariance $-\ (\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top+\sigma^2\mathbf{I})\rvert$ | $0.065452$ |
| the same, relative to the largest entry | $2.1\times10^{-3}$ |
| Monte-Carlo floor, $1/\sqrt{K}$ | $1.6\times10^{-3}$ |

The gap is sampling noise of the size you would predict, not a discrepancy.

The joint, using $\mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}] = \mathbf{B}\,\mathrm{Cov}_z[\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}] = \mathbf{B}$
(Equation 10.71):

$$
p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol\mu,\sigma^2)
= \mathcal{N}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\ \mathbf{z}\end{bmatrix}\ \Bigg|\
\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol\mu\\ \mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix},\
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top+\sigma^2\mathbf{I} & \mathbf{B}\\ \mathbf{B}^\top & \mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}\right)
\qquad \text{(10.72)}
$$

## §10.7.3 The posterior, and the thing it does not look at

Gaussian conditioning (§6.5.1) gives

$$
p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{m},\mathbf{C}) \qquad \text{(10.73)}
$$

$$
\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{B}^\top(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top+\sigma^2\mathbf{I})^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)
\qquad \text{(10.74)}
$$

$$
\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{B}^\top(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top+\sigma^2\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{B}
\qquad \text{(10.75)}
$$

> Note that the posterior covariance **does not depend on the observed data** $\mathbf{x}$.

:::tip[A closed form the book does not give, and what it says]
Equation 10.75 inverts a $D\times D$ matrix for an $M$-dimensional posterior — the same shape of
inefficiency page 908 found in Equation 9.64. Woodbury again:

$$
\mathbf{C} = \sigma^2\left(\mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{B} + \sigma^2\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}
$$

Measured against Equation 10.75: $9.7\times10^{-16}$. And now the structure is readable. For the
maximum-likelihood $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol\Lambda-\sigma^2\mathbf{I})^{1/2}$ we have
$\mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{B} = \boldsymbol\Lambda - \sigma^2\mathbf{I}$, so

$$
\mathbf{C} = \sigma^2\boldsymbol\Lambda^{-1}, \qquad C_{mm} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\lambda_m}
$$

| $m$ | measured $C_{mm}$ | $\sigma^2/\lambda_m$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | $0.026391$ | $0.026391$ |
| $2$ | $0.045603$ | $0.045603$ |
| $3$ | $0.053131$ | $0.053131$ |
| $4$ | $0.073999$ | $0.073999$ |
| $5$ | $0.076317$ | $0.076317$ |

Largest off-diagonal entry: $9.7\times10^{-16}$, and the mean $\mathbf{m}$ ranges over $0.7394$ to $3.1530$ in norm across the dataset while $\mathbf{C}$ never moves. **The posterior is uncertain in inverse proportion to
how much variance the axis carries** — which is the quantitative version of "the later components are
less reliable", and it needs no data to state.
:::

## §10.8's maximum-likelihood parameters

$$
\boldsymbol\mu_{\mathrm{ML}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{x}_n,
\qquad
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{ML}} = \mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol\Lambda-\sigma^2\mathbf{I})^{\frac12}\mathbf{R},
\qquad
\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}} = \frac{1}{D-M}\sum_{j=M+1}^{D}\lambda_j
\qquad \text{(10.77)–(10.79)}
$$

Equation 10.79 is page 1004's $J_M$ divided by the number of directions it is spread across — *"the
average leftover variance that we cannot capture with the first $M$ principal components is treated as
observation noise."* Measured:

| $M$ | $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}}$ | $J_M/(D-M)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | $9.35857873$ | $9.35857873$ |
| $5$ | $5.42581981$ | $5.42581981$ |
| $10$ | $3.07760746$ | $3.07760746$ |
| $20$ | $1.34890465$ | $1.34890465$ |
| $40$ | $0.12051166$ | $0.12051166$ |

Two consequences worth stating:

**$\mathbf{R}$ is back.** Equation 10.78 ends in *"an arbitrary orthogonal matrix"*, which page 1004
measured: rotating $\mathbf{B}$ leaves the reconstruction error at $320.123369$ and the projector
identical to $1.1\times10^{-16}$. Here it means the maximum-likelihood solution is a whole family, and
*"PCA can identify the best lower-dimensional subspace in which the signals live, but not the signals
themselves"* (§10.8). ICA exists to break that tie by making $p(\mathbf{z})$ non-Gaussian.

**The margin note is a guarantee, not an observation.** $\boldsymbol\Lambda-\sigma^2\mathbf{I}$ is
positive semi-definite because $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}}$ is an *average* of the discarded eigenvalues, and
the discarded ones are all below the kept ones by construction. The square root in Equation 10.78 is
always real.

## PPCA is PCA with shrinkage

Substituting $\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{ML}}$ into Equation 10.74 and reconstructing gives, per axis,

$$
\frac{(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{m})_m}{(\text{PCA projection})_m} = \frac{\lambda_m - \sigma^2}{\lambda_m}
$$

Measured against the prediction, four noise levels, five axes, and every cell matches:

| $\sigma^2$ | axis $1$ | axis $2$ | axis $3$ | axis $4$ | axis $5$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | $0.993402$ | $0.988599$ | $0.986717$ | $0.981500$ | $0.980921$ |
| $4$ | $0.973609$ | $0.954397$ | $0.946869$ | $0.926001$ | $0.923683$ |
| $16$ | $0.894437$ | $0.817589$ | $0.787474$ | $0.704006$ | $0.694733$ |
| $40$ | $0.736093$ | $0.543971$ | $0.468685$ | $0.260015$ | $0.236834$ |

:::caution[The noise-free limit is approached, and then floating point takes it back]
> In the noise-free limit where $\sigma \to 0$, PPCA and PCA provide identical solutions.

Measured, the ratio of PPCA's reconstruction to PCA's on the first axis:

| $\sigma^2$ | $\lVert\mathbf{m}$-reconstruction $-\ \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{PCA}}\rVert$ | ratio on axis $1$ | largest $\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{C})$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $10^{1}$ | $2.513944$ | $0.93402319$ | $1.9\times10^{-1}$ |
| $10^{0}$ | $0.251394$ | $0.99340232$ | $1.9\times10^{-2}$ |
| $10^{-2}$ | $0.002514$ | $0.99993402$ | $1.9\times10^{-4}$ |
| $10^{-6}$ | $0.000000$ | $1.00000004$ | $1.9\times10^{-8}$ |
| $10^{-10}$ | $\mathbf{0.000996}$ | $\mathbf{1.00024719}$ | $1.9\times10^{-12}$ |

The gap falls by a factor of $10$ for each factor of $10$ in $\sigma^2$ — until $10^{-10}$, where it
**rises again**. By then $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^\top + \sigma^2\mathbf{I}$ is numerically singular in the
$D-M$ directions $\mathbf{B}$ does not span, and inverting it stops meaning anything.

The limit is a mathematical statement, not a recipe. If you want PCA, run PCA; PPCA is for when
$\sigma^2$ is a quantity you actually want to estimate.
:::

## Recall card

- **Section 10.7 puts a probabilistic model behind PCA**, which buys a likelihood, model comparison, a generative story, missing-data handling and a notion of novelty — the same list Chapter 9 bought by going Bayesian.
- **The arrow points from the code to the data.** PPCA asserts a low-dimensional cause and then inverts it with Bayes' theorem, where plain PCA simply computed the code as a function of the data.
- **Integrating the latent variable out gives a Gaussian** with the sample mean and covariance B B-transpose plus sigma-squared I, confirmed by 400,000 ancestral samples to within the Monte-Carlo floor.
- **The cross-covariance between the data and the code is exactly B**, which is what makes the joint in Equation 10.72 write down so cleanly.
- **The posterior covariance does not depend on the observed data at all** — measured, exactly zero change over 200 observations, the same structure page 907 found in Bayesian linear regression.
- **Woodbury turns Equation 10.75 into an M-by-M inverse**, and then the structure is visible: for the maximum-likelihood B it is diagonal with entries sigma-squared over lambda-m.
- **So the posterior is uncertain in inverse proportion to the variance each axis carries**, and you know that before seeing any data.
- **Equation 10.79's noise variance is the average of the discarded eigenvalues** — page 1004's reconstruction error divided by the number of directions it is spread over.
- **Which is also why Equation 10.78's square root is always real:** an average of eigenvalues all below the kept ones is itself below them.
- **PPCA is PCA with shrinkage.** Each axis is scaled by lambda minus sigma-squared, over lambda — measured to six decimals across four noise levels, and hardest on the weakest axes.
- **As sigma goes to zero the two coincide**, but below about 1e-08 the matrix Equation 10.74 inverts is numerically singular. The limit is a statement, not a recipe.
- **The arbitrary orthogonal R in Equation 10.78 is page 1004's ambiguity again** — PCA finds the subspace, not the signals, which is why ICA changes the prior on the code instead.
