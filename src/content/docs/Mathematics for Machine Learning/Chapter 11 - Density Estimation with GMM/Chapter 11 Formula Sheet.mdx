## §11.1 The Gaussian mixture model

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| a mixture density | $p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k p_k(\mathbf{x})$ — **11.1** |
| the weights | $0 \leq \pi_k \leq 1$, $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$ — **11.2**, **both halves needed** |
| the GMM | $p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_k \pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$ — **11.3** |
| its weight constraints | $0 \leq \pi_k \leq 1$, $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$ — **11.4**, with $\boldsymbol\theta := \{\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k,\pi_k\}$ named in the text beneath it |
| the running example | $0.5\,\mathcal{N}(x\mid-2,\tfrac12) + 0.2\,\mathcal{N}(x\mid1,2) + 0.3\,\mathcal{N}(x\mid4,1)$ — **11.5** |
| the mean | $\mathbb{E}[x] = \sum_k \pi_k\mu_k$ — a weighted average |
| the variance | $\mathbb{V}[x] = \sum_k \pi_k\sigma_k^2 + \sum_k \pi_k(\mu_k-\mathbb{E}[x])^2$ — **not** a weighted average |

:::tip[The variance decomposition is the law of total variance, and it returns twice]
On Equation 11.5 the total is $7.79$: component widths supply $0.95$ and the spread of the means
supplies $6.84$, or $87.80\%$. Page 1105 shows this quantity is **conserved** by every M-step, so EM
only ever moves spread between the two terms.
:::

## §11.2 Maximum likelihood and the obstacle

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| the likelihood | $p(\mathcal{X}\mid\boldsymbol\theta) = \prod_n p(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\theta)$ — **11.9** |
| **the log-likelihood** | $L = \sum_{n}\log\sum_{k}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$ — **11.10** |
| the $K=1$ case | the log lands on the Gaussian and the answer is closed-form — **11.11** |
| the stationarity conditions | $\partial L/\partial\boldsymbol\mu_k = \mathbf{0}$, $\partial L/\partial\boldsymbol\Sigma_k = \mathbf{0}$, $\partial L/\partial\pi_k = 0$ — **11.12**–**11.14** |
| the chain rule | $\frac{\partial\log p(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\theta)}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta} = \frac{1}{p(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\theta)}\frac{\partial p(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\theta)}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}$ — **11.15** |
| the factor it drags in | $\frac{1}{p(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\theta)} = \big(\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)\big)^{-1}$ — **11.16** |

:::caution[The maximum likelihood estimate does not exist]
Pin a mean to a data point and let its variance shrink: Equation 11.10 reaches $+16.377668$ at
$\sigma^2 = 10^{-30}$ and has no upper bound. Of $400$ restarts on the book's seven points,
$\mathbf{218}$ — $54.5\%$ — collapse a component, and **every one reports a higher likelihood than
every genuine fit**.

Among the honest fits there are exactly two optima, and **the book's own initialisation reaches the
worse one**: $-13.973323$ against an available $-13.906162$.

§11.5 names this exactly — *"this happens when the mean of a mixture component is identical to a data
point and the covariance tends to $0$"* — and points at a Bayesian prior on the parameters. Page 1109
prices the alternative: a floor caps the singular branch, and each decade of floor is worth
$\tfrac12\ln 10 = 1.1513$ nats.
:::

## §11.2.1 Responsibilities

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| **the responsibility** | $r_{nk} := \dfrac{\pi_k\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)}$ — **11.17** |
| each row | $\mathbf{r}_n = [r_{n1},\ldots,r_{nK}]^\top$, a probability vector — **11.18** |
| the book's matrix | $\mathbf{R}$ for the seven points — **11.19** |
| **the effective count** | $N_k := \sum_n r_{nk}$, fractional, $\sum_k N_k = N$ — **11.24** |
| the data-side probability vector | $\mathbf{r}_k$ with entries $r_{nk}/N_k$ — **11.25** |
| as a softmax | $r_{nk} = e^{-E_{nk}}/\sum_j e^{-E_{nj}}$, $E_{nk} = \tfrac12(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k) + \tfrac12\log\det\boldsymbol\Sigma_k - \log\pi_k$ |

:::caution[Equation 11.17 as written returns NaN, and one entry of 11.19 is wrong]
Typed out literally, Equation 11.17 divides a vanishing number by a vanishing number: with small
variances it returns **NaN for all $21$ entries**. In log-space it returns the exact answer, and where
both work they agree to $2.2\times10^{-16}$.

The book's printed Equation 11.19 disagrees with the arithmetic at one entry. At $x = 0$ it prints
$0.001$ and $0.999$; the computed values are $\mathbf{0.000150}$ and $0.999844$. The largest
disagreement across the matrix is $0.001$.
:::

## §11.2.2 Updating the means

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| the Gaussian's $\boldsymbol\mu$-gradient | $\frac{\partial\mathcal{N}}{\partial\boldsymbol\mu_k} = (\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}\mathcal{N}$ — **11.21b** |
| assembled | $\frac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol\mu_k} = \sum_n r_{nk}(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}$ — **11.22c** |
| setting it to zero | $\sum_n r_{nk}\mathbf{x}_n = N_k\boldsymbol\mu_k^{\text{new}}$ — **11.23** |
| **Theorem 11.1** | $\boldsymbol\mu_k^{\text{new}} = \dfrac{\sum_n r_{nk}\mathbf{x}_n}{N_k}$ — **11.20** |
| as an expectation | $\boldsymbol\mu_k^{\text{new}} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{r}_k}[\mathcal{X}]$ — **11.26** |
| Example 11.3 | $-4 \to -2.701230$, $0 \to -0.403411$, $8 \to 3.704287$ — **11.27**–**11.29** |

:::tip[Two facts the book does not state]
**The update cannot leave the data's range.** It is a convex combination of the data whenever
$N_k > 0$, so $\min_n x_n \le \mu_k^{\text{new}} \le \max_n x_n$. Over $4{,}000$ starting values,
**zero excursions** — which makes it a sanity check on the responsibilities.

**The $N_k$-weighted sum of the new means is the sum of the data, exactly:**

$$
\sum_{k}N_k\boldsymbol\mu_k^{\text{new}} = \sum_k\sum_n r_{nk}\mathbf{x}_n = \sum_n \mathbf{x}_n\underbrace{\sum_k r_{nk}}_{=\,1} = \sum_n \mathbf{x}_n
$$

Measured over $2{,}000$ arbitrary responsibility matrices: worst deviation $3.6\times10^{-15}$. So the
mixture's mean is pinned to the sample mean after every M-step.
:::

## §11.2.3 Updating the covariances

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| the Gaussian's $\boldsymbol\Sigma$-gradient | $\frac{\partial p}{\partial\boldsymbol\Sigma_k} = \pi_k\mathcal{N}\cdot\left(-\tfrac12\big(\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}-\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}\big)\right)$ — **11.35** |
| stationarity | $N_k\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1} = \boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}\big(\sum_n r_{nk}(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top\big)\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}$ — **11.37a** |
| **Theorem 11.2** | $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{\text{new}} = \dfrac{1}{N_k}\sum_n r_{nk}(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top$ — **11.30** |
| as an expectation | an importance-weighted mean of $\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_k = \{\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k\}$ — **11.38** |
| Example 11.4 | $1 \to 0.14$, $0.2 \to 0.44$, $3 \to 1.53$ — **11.39**–**11.41** |

:::caution[Equation 11.30 does not say which mean it uses, and it matters]
| $\boldsymbol\mu$ used | result |
| --- | --- |
| the **old** means | $1.83$, $0.60$, $19.98$ |
| the **just-updated** means | $\mathbf{0.14}$, $\mathbf{0.44}$, $\mathbf{1.53}$ — the book's Example 11.4 |

The M-step's ordering is the answer: §11.3 updates the means first. Stale means measure spread about a
point the component has already left, and on the third component that is a factor of $13.0878$.
:::

:::tip[An invariant stronger than the mean's]
After **any** M-step the mixture's variance equals the sample variance, exactly:

$$
\sum_k \pi_k^{\text{new}}\left((\sigma_k^{\text{new}})^2 + (\mu_k^{\text{new}})^2\right) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n x_n^2
$$

Measured to $3.6\times10^{-15}$ over $20{,}000$ arbitrary responsibility matrices, for every $K$ from
$2$ to $10$. **EM cannot make the model wider or narrower than the data** — it only decides how much
spread is between components and how much is within them. On the book's example the total drops to
$8.336735$ at the first M-step and never moves again.
:::

## §11.2.4 Updating the mixture weights

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| the Lagrangian | $\mathfrak{L} = L + \lambda\big(\sum_k\pi_k - 1\big)$ — **11.43a** |
| the derivative | $\frac{\partial\mathfrak{L}}{\partial\pi_k} = \frac{N_k}{\pi_k} + \lambda$ — **11.44b** |
| the constraint | $\frac{\partial\mathfrak{L}}{\partial\lambda} = \sum_k\pi_k - 1 = 0$ — **11.45** |
| solving | $\pi_k = -N_k/\lambda$ — **11.46**, then $\sum_k N_k = N$ — **11.47** |
| **the multiplier** | $\lambda = -N$ — **11.48** |
| **Theorem 11.3** | $\pi_k^{\text{new}} = \dfrac{N_k}{N}$ — **11.42** |
| Example 11.5 | $\tfrac13 \to 0.293890,\ 0.287001,\ 0.419109$ — the book's $0.29$, $0.29$, $0.42$ |

:::note[Why this one needs a multiplier and the other two do not]
$\partial L/\partial\pi_k = \sum_n \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)\big/\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j) > 0$
in **every** coordinate, so unconstrained ascent has nothing to converge to — measured, the weights'
sum reaches $2.54$ in six steps and keeps climbing. The means and covariances are unconstrained; the
weights live on a simplex.

Contrast §10.2's Lagrangian, where the multiplier **was** the answer — an eigenvalue, equal to the
variance retained. Here $\lambda = -N$ is the normalising constant and carries no information.
:::

## §11.3 The EM algorithm

| step | equation |
| --- | --- |
| initialise | $\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k,\pi_k$ |
| **E-step** | $r_{nk} = \dfrac{\pi_k\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)}$ — **11.53** |
| **M-step**, in this order | $\boldsymbol\mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n r_{nk}\mathbf{x}_n$ — **11.54** |
| | $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n r_{nk}(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top$ — **11.55** |
| | $\pi_k = N_k/N$ — **11.56** |
| the guarantee | $L$ is non-decreasing at every step (Neal and Hinton, 1999) |

| measured on the book's example | value |
| --- | --- |
| negative log-likelihood, start to first cycle | $28.325536 \to 14.410485$ — the book's $28.3$ and $14.4$ |
| Example 11.6's "after five iterations" | the $10^{-6}$ criterion, reproduced exactly; $8$ at $10^{-9}$ |
| $L$ settles before the parameters do | $5$ against $11$ iterations at $10^{-6}$ |

:::caution[What the monotonicity guarantee looks like in float64]
Over $180{,}000$ steps, $\mathbf{11{,}163}$ register a **negative** change in $L$. None is larger than
$8.9\times10^{-15}$, and the median decrease is $1.776\times10^{-15}$ — **exactly one unit in the last
place** of a log-likelihood near $-14$.

The theorem is about real numbers. A convergence test must therefore compare $|\Delta L|$ against a
tolerance, never against zero.
:::

:::tip[Overlap sets the rate, and the initialisation sets the tail]
EM converges linearly, and the constant is the ambiguity of the responsibilities: $1045$ iterations
when $53\%$ of points are ambiguous against $3$ when none are — a factor of $\mathbf{348}$. When
$r_{nk} \approx \tfrac1K$ the E-step barely moves, so the M-step barely moves.

A K-means initialisation cuts the median from $58$ iterations to $19$, removes the long tail, and takes
the share of runs reaching the best optimum from $96\%$ to $100\%$. It does **not** make the optimum
global — nothing can outrun an unbounded objective.
:::

## §11.4 The latent variable perspective

| what | equation |
| --- | --- |
| the indicator | $\mathbf{z} = [z_1,\ldots,z_K]^\top$, one-hot — defined in the text of §11.4.1, unnumbered |
| the conditional | $p(\mathbf{x}\mid z_k=1) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$ — **11.58** |
| the prior | $p(\mathbf{z}) = \boldsymbol\pi$, $\pi_k = p(z_k=1)$ — **11.59**, **11.60** |
| the joint, one component | $p(\mathbf{x},z_k=1) = \pi_k\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$ — **11.61** |
| the joint, stacked | the $K$-vector of those products — **11.62**, "which fully specifies the probabilistic model" |
| marginalising $\mathbf{z}$ | $p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_{\mathbf{z}}p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf{z})p(\mathbf{z}\mid\boldsymbol\theta)$, recovering Equation 11.3 — **11.63**, **11.65**, **11.66b** |
| the dataset likelihood | $p(\mathcal{X}\mid\boldsymbol\theta) = \prod_n\sum_k\pi_k\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$ — **11.67**, i.e. Equation 11.9 again |
| **the posterior** | $p(z_k=1\mid\mathbf{x}) = \dfrac{\pi_k\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)}$ — **11.69** |
| the conditional factorises | $p(\mathbf{x}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{x}_N\mid\mathbf{z}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{z}_N) = \prod_n p(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\mathbf{z}_n)$ — **11.71** |
| **and over a dataset** | $p(z_{nk}=1\mid\mathbf{x}_n) = r_{nk}$ — **11.72b**, the identification the chapter was built toward |
| **the E-step's object** | $Q(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\boldsymbol\theta^{(t)}) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\theta^{(t)}}[\log p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\mid\boldsymbol\theta)]$ — **11.73a** |
| the M-step | $\boldsymbol\theta^{(t+1)} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta} Q(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\boldsymbol\theta^{(t)})$ |

:::tip[What §11.4 buys that §11.2 cannot]
**The responsibility is a posterior, exactly.** Computing the joint over the marginal reproduces
Equation 11.17 to $0.0$ — not approximately. That is what licenses summing responsibilities as counts,
differentiating a soft assignment, and alternating at all.

**The M-step is the exact argmax of $Q$**, not a heuristic: a forty-restart Nelder–Mead search matches
it to $5.3\times10^{-15}$, with every parameter agreeing to six decimals.

**$L = Q + H(\mathbf{r})$**, measured to $1.1\times10^{-14}$, so $Q$ is a lower bound that is tight at
the current parameters — and raising a tight lower bound cannot lower what it bounds, which is §11.3's
guarantee with a reason attached. $L$ rises by **more** than $Q$ does, $13.915050$ against $13.480070$,
because the new responsibilities are sharper and the entropy grows too.
:::

## The chapter in twelve lines

1. **A mixture buys multimodality for a handful of parameters** — $1.388610$ nats per point for twelve of them — and pays with the closed form.
2. **Equation 11.2 needs both halves.** Weights that sum to one but go negative still integrate to one and are still not a density.
3. **The variance of a mixture is not the average of the variances**, and that decomposition is conserved by every M-step.
4. **Equation 11.10's log sits outside a sum**, so the chain rule drags $1/p(\mathbf{x}_n)$ into every derivative and every parameter's equation mentions every other.
5. **Equation 11.20 is a fixed point, not a formula.** Feeding it back in moves the means by $4.295713$.
6. **The responsibility is that dragged-in factor, named** — and §11.4 shows it is a posterior, exactly.
7. **All three updates are the same derivation:** weighted average of the data, of the squared deviations, of nothing at all.
8. **Only the weights need a Lagrange multiplier**, because only they are constrained — and it comes out as $-N$, carrying no information.
9. **Order the M-step.** Equation 11.30 with stale means is wrong by a factor of $13.0878$ on the book's third component.
10. **EM's guarantee holds to one ULP**, so test convergence against a tolerance and never against zero.
11. **The maximum likelihood estimate does not exist.** A floor prices the singularity at $1.1513$ nats per decade; a prior removes it.
12. **§11.5's "$K$ is known" is doing the heaviest lifting in the chapter** — held-out likelihood is not even a consistent estimator of it.
