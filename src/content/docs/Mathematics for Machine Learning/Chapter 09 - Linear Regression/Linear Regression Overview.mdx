Chapter 8 built a vocabulary and named three flavours of learning. **Chapter 9 applies all three to one
problem**, in order, and the problem is the first of the book's four pillars: *"we aim to find a function
$f$ that maps inputs $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^D$ to corresponding function values $f(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}$."*

The book's framing: *"we will apply the mathematical concepts from Chapters 2, 5, 6, and 7 to solve linear
regression (curve fitting) problems"* — and the task is *"to find a function that not only models the
training data, but generalizes well."*

## One problem, three times

| | §9.2.1 maximum likelihood | §9.2.3 MAP | §9.3 Bayesian |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| what you write down | a **likelihood** | likelihood $\times$ **prior** | the same, and you **integrate** |
| the answer | a point | a point | a **distribution** |
| exists when $K > N$ | no | yes | yes |
| predictive interval | width $\sigma$, everywhere | width $\sigma$, everywhere | widens off the data |
| measured coverage | $0.7419$ / $0.1112$ | the same | $\mathbf{0.9487}$ / $\mathbf{0.9499}$ |

**Two of the three give the same prediction curve.** Measured to $8.4\times10^{-14}$: MAP and the
Bayesian posterior mean are one curve, because for a Gaussian the mode is the mean. So the case for §9.3
is not about the line — it is entirely about the shading, and the shading is what turns $0.1112$ into
$0.9499$.

## Why this chapter is where the measuring pays off

Everything here has a closed form, so every claim can be checked exactly rather than approximately. Four
things the chapter states, and what measuring them turned up:

**The book gives two different $\lambda$ for one correspondence.** Equation 9.33's text says
$\lambda = 1/(2b^2)$ and Equation 9.34's says $\lambda = \sigma^2/b^2$. Measured across five settings,
only the second reproduces Equation 9.31 — and Chapter 8 supplies a **third**, $\sigma^2/(N\tau^2)$, for
its own normalisation. All three are internally correct. **A $\lambda$ is meaningless without the
objective it belongs to.**

**Equation 9.22's noise estimate is biased by exactly $(N-K)/N$.** The book states it without caveat.
Measured at $N = 10$, $K = 5$ — Example 9.5's own setting — it returns **half** the true variance, and
§9.4's geometry explains why: $\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{I}-P) = N-K$, so the residual never occupies all $N$
dimensions.

**Example 9.7's prior is far wider than its figure can show.** With the book's own
$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \tfrac14\mathbf{I})$ and degree-5 monomials, the $95\%$ band is $3125.59$ units
wide at $x = 5$ — on axes drawn from $-4$ to $4$. It fits inside them at $48$ of the $200$ plotted
inputs. The shape the figure draws is right; the scale is the part worth measuring.

**Equation 9.64 is a derivation, not an implementation.** It inverts an $N\times N$ matrix for a model
with $K$ parameters. Woodbury gives the same number through a $K\times K$ one — $4341\times$ faster at
$N = 4000$ — and its determinant factor is $\mathbf{S}_N^{-1}$, already computed by Theorem 9.1.

## The thread that runs through it

One number appears on three pages by three different routes, and noticing that is most of understanding
Bayes' theorem in this setting:

| where it appears | page | value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the constant offset making Theorem 9.1 consistent | 906 | $+30.453407$ |
| the training targets' density under the prior predictive | 907 | $-30.453407$ |
| Equation 9.64 evaluated directly | 908 | $-30.453407$ |

It is $\log p(\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X})$ — the normaliser page 901 found missing when the likelihood
turned out to integrate to $1/\lvert x\rvert$ rather than $1$. Maximum likelihood can ignore it because an
argmax ignores scale. **Everything else in the chapter needs it**, and §8.6 needs it to compare models at
all.

## What is actually new here

Chapter 8 established the ideas. This chapter supplies the closed forms — and three results that only
appear once you have them:

**Precisions add, so learning can be sequential.** Equation 9.43b is a sum over observations, so feeding
the data in any order or grouping gives the same posterior. Measured over $500$ points fed one at a time:
$6.8\times10^{-15}$ from the batch answer, in $O(K^2)$ memory, with the design matrix never assembled.
The book computes it in one shot; nothing requires that.

**The error bars are known before the experiment.** $\mathbf{S}_N$ contains no $\mathbf{y}$ — measured,
shuffling the targets changes it by exactly zero. So you can choose where to measure to minimise the
interval where it matters: a clustered design gives a worst $95\%$ half-width of $286.95$ against
$0.474$ for a spread one, **a factor of $605$**, decided in advance. The same property is why the shading
does not widen when the model fits badly.

**The estimator is a projection, and that explains two earlier puzzles.** $\mathrm{tr}(P) = K$ gives
Equation 9.22's bias exactly; and since a projection depends on the *subspace* rather than the basis,
page 902's conditioning catastrophe ($\kappa = 2.2\times10^{26}$ for monomials against $1.000000$ for an
orthonormal basis, same fit to $4.8\times10^{-13}$) was always a coordinate problem.

## Recall card

- **Chapter 9 solves one problem three times** — maximum likelihood, MAP, and full Bayesian inference — applying Chapter 8's three flavours of learning to the first of the four pillars.
- **Two of the three give the same prediction curve**, to 8.4e-14, because for a Gaussian the mode is the mean. The case for Section 9.3 is entirely about the interval.
- **And the interval is what turns 0.1112 into 0.9499** in measured coverage just outside the data.
- **The book names two different lambdas for one correspondence**, and Chapter 8 supplies a third. All are internally correct; a lambda is meaningless without its objective.
- **Equation 9.22's noise estimate is biased by exactly (N-K)/N**, returning half the truth in Example 9.5's own setting. Section 9.4 explains why: the trace of I minus P is N minus K.
- **Example 9.7's prior implies a 95 percent band 3125.59 units wide at x = 5**, on a figure whose axes run from minus four to four.
- **Equation 9.64 inverts an N-by-N matrix for a K-parameter model.** Woodbury gives the same number 4341 times faster, reusing the posterior precision Theorem 9.1 already computed.
- **One number appears on three pages by three routes:** 30.453407, the log marginal likelihood — the normaliser page 901 found missing.
- **Precisions add, so the posterior update runs online** in fixed memory, matching the batch answer to 6.8e-15 over 500 points.
- **The error bars never see the targets**, so they can be minimised during experimental design — a factor of 605 between a clustered and a spread layout — and they cannot warn you that the model is wrong.
- **The estimator is an orthogonal projection**, which explains both the noise-variance bias and why the monomial basis's conditioning disaster was avoidable.
- **Section 9.5 points to generalized linear models, Gaussian processes and sparsity priors**, and the chapter has already computed a piece of each.
